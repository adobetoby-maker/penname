# Chapter Ten

What Rook had told them, at the fire, the night before, with a stick in his hand and all four of them sitting down and nobody bleeding, was this:

"They stand about as high as me, and about nine-tenths of it is leg. Two legs, jointed backwards, longer than they've any business being, and a body slung between them no bigger than a dog, and a head you won't be able to find and won't need to. Colour of wet ash, same as the hounds, same as everything.

"Husks come at you. Hounds choose a side and commit and can't take it back. Learn both of those, they're good, they'll keep you alive for years.

"These do neither, and I want you to understand why, because the why is the only part you'll be able to use. There's nothing in them that has to gather itself up. A dog crouches. A man leans. Anything that has to build speed has to start building it somewhere, and that somewhere is the thing I've spent two weeks teaching you to watch for. These don't build it. Stopped and going flat out are the same state to them, and there is no instant in between. If you wait to see it start, it has finished.

"So don't watch them. Watch the *lines*.

"They go in straight lines, and they can't bend one once they're in it. They have to stop to choose again, and when they stop, they're worth nothing, and that's the whole of your chance. The orchard's planted in rows across the slope. A row is a line. The ditch at the bottom of the orchard is a line. The gap where the fence is down is a line. Anywhere you can see a long way, something can come the whole way in no time at all; anywhere there's a tree trunk between you and it, it has to stop first.

"Stand where the trees are. Never stand at the end of a row. If you find yourself looking down a long clear stretch of anything, you are already in the wrong place, and you should move before you finish the thought."

Then he had said, "Say it back," and made them, one at a time, all four, until Milo could do it in his sleep, and Wyck had done it twice in a voice of enormous patience.

Toren had gone to sleep with his hand on a warm stone thinking that this was the easiest thing anyone had ever asked him to remember.

---

They saw the hedge thing on the track out, about two miles in, and it did Toren more damage than anything else that happened that day.

It was in the thorn hedge on the north side of the farm track, sixty feet off: a low grey shape that came out of the scrub at a lope, and stopped.

Toren's whole spine went cold, and his mouth was already moving.

"*Right*," he said. "One right, in the hedge, it's gone still, it's choosing — it's going to come and it's coming right, everybody right—"

They turned right. Wyck planted. Milo got the call out clean behind him. Rook stepped out onto the verge, and the verge-hound came out of the thorn at them in a flat silent rush at the exact angle Toren had called, and met four people who were already facing it and a man who took its front leg off at the shoulder in one movement, and there was a second one behind it that broke off before it had committed and went away along the hedge line and did not come back.

Thirty seconds. Nobody touched.

"Well," said Dessa, from the cart.

Milo was laughing in the high way he had when he had been frightened. "He *called* it. Tor called it before it moved."

"He called it right," said Rook.

And he did not say anything else, and Toren walked the next two miles with his ears ringing and his chest about four inches further out than it had been, and every step of those two miles laid down a little more of the thing that was going to cost them at four o'clock.

---

The orchard was on the south slope above the creek, and it had not been picked in sixty years.

It ran about two hundred yards across the hill and seven rows down it, old trees grown together over their own windfall, and the rows were as straight as the day somebody had pegged them out. Along the bottom, between the lowest row and the creek bank, ran a stone-lined ditch four feet deep and as straight as the rows. At the western end stood the press-house: one room of dressed stone with the roof fallen in and one wall standing to eight feet, a doorway in it, and three worn steps going up to the doorway from the orchard floor.

"Cart there," said Rook, pointing at the press-house. "Against the wall, mule tied short. Dessa, up on the steps with your grandmother — up on the top step, so you can see over the rows. You call what you see and don't call what you think it means."

"Yes."

"Senna, you stay in that cart whatever happens and you keep your head below the boards."

"I've been in this cart since Sallow Creek," said Senna. "It's the only thing in my life I'm actually good at."

"Milo, light in your hands the whole time, and you keep the pair of them behind you." He turned. "Wyck, Toren. Third row down, the pair of you, at the thick end where the trunks are close. Not the fourth. The fourth's got the fence gap at the end of it."

"Where are you?" said Toren.

"Where I need to be."

---

There were four of them in the orchard, which was what Rook had said there would be.

The first one Toren saw was standing three rows down and eighty feet along, between two trees, and it was so still and so exactly the colour of the grey trunks that he looked straight through it twice.

Then it was thirty feet closer.

There was no motion between the two positions. Toren's eye did not record a journey; it recorded one thing and then another thing, and the only evidence that any travel had occurred was the sound, which came half an instant late and which Toren knew, and which turned his stomach over: a series of hard flat cracks, spaced, like a stone skipping across water.

He had heard it exactly once before, coming down a rock face forty feet above a road, out of a man he did not yet know.

"*Third row, two of them, third row!*" — Dessa, from the steps, clear and hard.

"Two in the row," Milo called, "one at forty, one at sixty—"

"Two in the row, one at forty," Toren said after him, and got the spike out, and it came out warm.

Then the row discipline worked, and it worked so well that it was almost boring.

That was the shape of the next four minutes. A Stilt at the end of a row was a straight line, and a straight line was a killing thing, so they did not stand in rows. They stood *across* them, in the thick of the trunks where the old trees had grown into each other, and every time one of the things wanted to reach them, it had to stop out in the open to choose a new line, and the instant it stopped, it was a two-legged thing standing on the grass doing nothing.

Wyck killed the first one with the bar across the knee of its left leg while it stood picking its way through the geometry, and the whole creature went down like a stepladder coming apart.

Toren killed the second one poorly and in three goes, and would have been in trouble if Rook had not come through the row behind him with no warning at all and taken its other leg off.

"Trunks," Rook said, and was gone.

"I *am* on the trunks—"

"You're a foot off them."

The third one broke off and went up the slope into the windbreak and did not come back down.

Toren stood in the third row with his heart going and a warm spike in his fist and thought: *we're doing it. We're actually doing it.*

---

Dessa said, "*Ditch.*"

She said it in a completely different voice from the one she had been using, and it went across the orchard like a thrown stone.

"Ditch — the ditch, the ditch, there's things in the *ditch* — three — four, four in the ditch and they're coming up it, they're coming *west* —"

West was the press-house.

West was the cart.

Toren turned and looked down the slope and could see nothing at all, because the ditch was four feet deep and stone-lined and dead straight, and anything in it was invisible from the third row and was travelling along the cleanest line on that entire hillside.

He had heard Rook say *the ditch at the bottom of the orchard is a line*. He had said it back, out loud, at a fire, the night before. He had understood it perfectly, and it had gone into him next to nine other things, and he had spent the last four minutes thinking about rows.

"*Down!*" Rook shouted, from somewhere up the slope. "Everyone west — press-house — *now*, go, go—"

They went. Running downhill across old orchard rows with windfall under the grass is not running, it is a controlled fall with interruptions, and Toren went over twice and kept the spike both times and did not look at his hand.

The Stilts came out of the ditch at the western end, where the stone gave out, and the bank shelved, and they came out one behind another, and the ground between the ditch mouth and the press-house steps was the one piece of open mown-flat grass in the whole orchard.

Rook got there first by an enormous margin. Toren heard the struts fire twice and did not see the man cross any of it.

"Line!" Rook shouted. "On me — *across* the open, not along it —"

They made a line eleven feet in front of the press-house steps, with the open grass in front of them and the fallen wall at their backs and a trodden windfall floor under their feet, and for about ninety seconds it was the best thing the four of them had ever done together.

Milo had the light up over his head and did not drop it. Dessa called from the top step with the whole slope under her eye and got every single one right. Wyck planted at the left end because the left end was where the ditch mouth was, and he did not have to be told. Toren held the middle and said everything Milo said one beat behind him, louder, and twice Rook did what the middle said.

They killed two more on the open grass. Rook took one in the air, which Toren had not known was possible and which he thought about for years.

And then the fourth one stopped at the mouth of the ditch, forty feet out, side-on, with its body down low between the long legs.

It stood absolutely still.

Toren's whole spine went cold — and the cold arrived with a name on it, and the name was a gift, and it was the wrong gift, and it was his.

"*Right!*" he shouted. "It's chosen — it's going right, it's committed right, turn right, everybody *right*—"

Milo said, "It hasn't—"

"It's the wind-up, Milo, it's *going*—"

And the line turned right, because Toren was in the middle, and the middle was where the calls came from.

The thing at the ditch mouth was not winding up. It was not doing anything. It had stopped in the open to choose a line, exactly as Rook had said it would, and the line it chose was the one the four of them had just opened for it by turning, and there was no instant between the choosing and the arriving.

It came through the left end of the line at the speed of a dropped stone, and it did not touch anybody, because there was nobody there any more, and behind it the ditch mouth put up another one.

Two of them, inside the line, between the line and the press-house steps, and on the steps there was a boy with a light in his hands, a girl who could not run, and an old woman in a cart.

---

Toren did not decide anything.

That was what he told Dessa afterward, and it was true, and it was not the whole truth. He did not decide; but for four days, he had been carrying around a thing he had been told to carry — *stop auditing it* — and for two weeks, he had been carrying a thing he had watched: a girl on the other side of a fire with a stone going warm in her hands, saying *it's not in the stone*.

What he did was stop asking.

He did not ask the Ember for anything. He did not hold it harder. He did not look at his hand. He turned and went after the nearer of the two with the whole of himself out in front of him and nothing whatsoever held back, and somewhere behind his own eyes a door that had been standing an inch open since a road cut came the rest of the way open, and everything behind it came through at once.

The spike changed.

He felt it go. It ran out of his fist in a single clean movement, thinner and half again as long and not ugly at all, and the whole of his forearm went to light, and the world went very simple, and he put it through the first Stilt lengthwise and took both legs off the second one on the backswing, and neither of them touched the ground before he had turned for a third.

There was no third.

There was nothing at all. The bottom came out of him the way the bottom comes out of a wet sack.

He was on his knees. He had not been aware of any part of that. The spike was gone — not withdrawn, not put away, simply not there — and the grey slab in his fist was cold, cold the way the inside of a well is cold, cold the way it had been on every morning of his life before this one, and his arms would not lift and his ears were full of a high flat sound and he could not make his head come up off his chest.

*Get up*, he thought.

He watched his own hand lie in the grass in front of him and do nothing.

*Get up. Get up. Left end. There's nothing on the left end.*

---

There was something on the left end.

Wyck Tanner stepped across the gap where Toren should have been, which meant standing at the only place on that whole hillside where the ditch mouth had a clean straight run at the press-house steps, and he set his feet, and he did not move.

Toren saw it from the ground, four feet away, with his cheek in the windfall.

The Stilt came out of the ditch mouth at the speed those things came out of everything, and it hit Wyck square, and there was a sound like a barrow going over on a stone floor, and Wyck did not go anywhere.

He should have. That was the part Toren could not make sit down flat in his head afterward, no matter how many times he went over it. Nine stone of boy, and a thing that had crossed forty feet in no time at all, and it came off him like water off a gatepost, and Wyck's boots were exactly where Wyck's boots had been. It opened his left forearm from the elbow to the wrist on the way past, and he did not appear to notice, and he swung the bar into its trailing leg as it went and broke the leg, and the thing went down on the steps, and Milo Faust kicked it in the head with both heels, screaming, from above, the way he had kicked one off a cart bed thirteen days ago.

The last one came out of the ditch at Wyck and did the same thing, and got the same result.

He held the gap for about eleven seconds.

Rook came down the line and finished all of it, and stopped, and stood in the open grass with his head turning once, twice, and then let his arms down.

The orchard went quiet.

Milo's light was still up.

---

The ash ran down the mown grass and pooled in the shallow places, and Rook let it lie for eleven minutes, which nobody had ever seen him do, because he was working on Wyck's arm.

He worked on it for a long time. He had Dessa hold the lamp — she had come down the steps on the stick without asking anybody and had got herself onto one knee in the grass and stayed there — and Milo with two hands where he was put. He had Wyck's arm across his own knee, his Ember against the inside of the elbow, his palm flat over the length of the cut, and after a while the bleeding slowed the way water slows when somebody upstream shuts a gate part of the way.

Then he sat back on his heels and got the flat tin flask out and looked at what was in it and drank all of it.

"That's not fixed," he said. "That's slowed. It wants stitching, and I haven't got the thread for a cut that long, so it's going to knit ugly, and it's going to be stiff, and you're going to have it for the rest of your life." He wound the rag. "You'll use that hand tomorrow. Not well. Tomorrow."

"Right," said Wyck.

"Say if you go cold or the fingers go stupid. Say it out loud when it happens, not an hour after."

"Right."

Rook tied the rag off and did not let go of the arm.

"Now the other thing," he said.

"What other thing?"

"Two of them hit you at full run and you're standing where you were standing."

Wyck said nothing.

"How long?" said Rook.

"How long what?"

"How long have you been able to do it."

"I can't *do* anything," said Wyck, and his voice came apart slightly in the middle of it, which was the first time Toren had ever heard that happen. "I haven't got anything. I'm fifteen and nothing, ask the woman with the book, she wrote it down. I just don't —"

"Don't move," said Rook.

"— don't fall over," said Wyck.

"No. You don't."

Rook let go of the arm.

"That's Ember," he said. "That's what it's called, and that's what you are as of about four o'clock this afternoon, and I'd guess you were most of the way there on a tailgate in a road cut thirteen days ago with a bit of rebar, before anybody here had taught you a thing.

"And I'll tell you the part nobody in a settlement will ever tell you, because a settlement can't see it. There are three doors. One of them's a stone somebody hands you at thirteen and it does a trick and everyone can watch. One of them's the other business, the wall, the wound, the flask. And one of them is nothing anybody can look at. It doesn't come out of an object, and it doesn't come in from outside. It's a person deciding, over and over, for years, in a lot of small places where it didn't matter, not to be moved — and then one day it isn't a decision any more, it's just true about them, and a thing that crosses forty feet in no time at all comes off them like weather off a wall.

"They'll never put you in a book for it. You can't show it to anybody. You can only be it, and you have been being it since before I met you, and it's the least impressive and most useful thing on this hillside."

Wyck sat on the press-house step with his arm across his knees and his face turned away, and did not say anything at all for a long moment.

Then he said, thickly, "Toren went first."

"Yes."

"He went in front of me. Again."

"Yes," said Rook. "And he's face down in the windfall and you're sitting up, so let's neither of us pretend that settles anything."

---

Toren could not stand until nearly dark.

That was the part nobody talked about on the way back, and it was the part he hated most. He lay in the orchard grass with the whole of the hillside going on around him — the ash being lifted, the mule being calmed, his grandmother's voice going up and down over the press-house wall in a register of pure fury — and could not get his arms to do anything. When he finally got up, he had to be walked to the cart with Dessa's shoulder under one arm and Milo's under the other, which was, as Milo pointed out, the single most humiliating configuration available to any of the three of them.

They put him in the cart. With the two invalids.

"Well," said Senna. "Look at this."

"Don't."

"*Three* of us."

Rook came to the tailgate and put two fingers on the inside of Toren's wrist, and held them there a long time.

"Cold," he said.

"It's never been cold."

"It's been cold your whole life until yesterday afternoon, and you'd stopped noticing after one day. That's how fast it happens." He let go. "There's nothing wrong with you. There's nothing in you. It isn't the same thing, and it'll take a week for you to stop confusing them."

"A week?"

"Two days before you can hold the spike. A week before it's worth anything." Rook leaned on the tailgate. "You'll fill again on your own, at the speed a person fills, which is slow and narrow, and it's the only speed there is until somebody shows you otherwise."

"Can you—"

"I could borrow you some," said Rook, "off her, or off him, or off the boy with the light, and then they'd be the ones lying in the cart. That's the entire trade, and it doesn't get better with practice."

"No," said Toren.

"No," Rook agreed.

---

The debrief was that night, in the lee of the windbreak, with the lamp on the cart shaft and all five of them where he could see them.

Toren waited for it with something close to nausea. He knew the shape of this. He had stood on a bridge in the fog eight days ago and had the shape of it laid over him like a flat stone, and he had deserved it then, and he deserved it a great deal more now, and he had spent the whole walk back composing the only sentence he had, which was *I did it again*.

Rook said: "Right things first.

"The girl on the steps called the ditch. She called it four seconds after anything was visible from where she was standing, which is as fast as it could be called by anybody, and she called what she saw and not what she thought it meant, and if she'd been wrong, we'd have run west for nothing, and I'd have said *good* then too.

"The boy kept a light over his head with his arms shaking for eleven minutes, and called every position he was given, and got none of them wrong. He hasn't got one wrong in fifteen days.

"The one with the bar held a gap on a bad arm, alone, twice, with nobody in support, having stepped into it on his own judgment while the person whose gap it was lay in front of him. Nobody told him to, and nobody had time to.

"And the one in the middle went at two of them, alone, in the two seconds when there was nobody else who could, and killed both, and I want all four of you to hear me say that before I say the rest, because the rest is going to take some time."

Nobody moved.

"Now," said Rook. "The call at the ditch mouth."

"I know," said Toren.

"Say it."

"I said it was a wind-up. It wasn't. It was standing there." His voice was flat and awful in his own ears. "You told me. You told all of us, last night, sitting down, in words, and I said it back to you twice. *There's no instant in between.* I said that back to you out loud." He pressed his hands into his eyes. "And I did it anyway. And I turned the line, and Wyck's arm—"

"Yes."

"So what was the *point*?" It came out of him louder than he meant it, and Milo flinched. "What was the point of telling us? You said you wouldn't, you said you keep the tell back on purpose and it costs a leg and that's the trade and you'd make it again — and then you told us, and it *still* — so what was it for?"

Rook was quiet for a moment.

"Because you turned the line for two seconds," he said, "instead of four.

"That's all it bought. That's what it was for. On the bridge you were wrong for as long as it took a girl to get three hounds out of a treeline, because you had no other picture in your head to fall back on. Tonight you had one, and you didn't believe it fast enough, and you were wrong for two seconds instead of four, and the difference between two and four is why he's got a forearm, and she's got a leg that limps.

"Knowing a thing and doing it while frightened are two separate skills. I said that to you on a bridge, and it's still true. What I had wrong—"

He stopped.

He was standing at the edge of the lamplight with his hands in his coat pockets, and he stood there for a second and a half with the sentence unfinished in the air, and Toren, watching him, thought: *he's never done that either.*

"Both of them are trainable," said Rook. "That's all. Go to sleep."

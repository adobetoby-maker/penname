# Shakedown Test 1 — The Feryn Exhibition (fresh rendering from card)

They chalked the circle fresh for it. That was the first sign the evening belonged to nobody's ordinary ledger: Vell never wasted chalk on a match no one would dispute.

Cael stood at the western edge of the packed-dirt circle behind the Cinder House and rolled his shoulders one at a time, because a routine kept was a thing nerves could hold on to, and his nerves needed the work. The yard rose around the fight ground in a shallow ring — barrel-tops, benches, the low wall along the alley side — and every place a person could stand or lean held one. He recognized most of the faces. That was the trouble. The purse Feryn had posted would have pulled a crowd in any district; what packed the wall was that everyone who knew Cael's name had come to watch that name get weighed against a Bronze.

Vell sat at the eastern edge on his stool, ledger open across his knees, pen capped until there was something worth writing. He had read the terms aloud twice already — exhibition rules, purse to the winner, circuit points recorded for Feryn's tour — and now he simply waited, which was Vell's way of saying the talking had ended.

Feryn crossed the chalk from the east and the crowd noise dropped a register without anyone hushing it. He moved the way water moved downhill, no wasted correction anywhere, and he looked at Cael with the mild, cataloguing interest of a man confirming an appointment. Twenty-four, Bronze Rank Two, ten years and two full tiers of distance between them. He had arranged this tour himself, Vell said — testing his edges before his own evaluation — and an untiered local made a reasonable evening's work on the way to somewhere that mattered.

Three weeks of Corvane's framework sat in Cael's chest like borrowed furniture. Buildup precedes release. The release moment can be read. Corvane had drilled it on slow demonstration and paper diagrams, never once at speed, and Cael had won his two prep matches against a Copper Rank Three quickly enough that the framework never got tested past its first page. The wind-thing — Lira's wind, the fragment that had come from watching her and belonged to him now in the way a limp belongs to a leg — he had tried to summon it in the empty yard that morning, forty times, and it had come twice, both times wrong. He had stopped trying. It surfaced when it wanted. Counting on it would be like counting on rain.

So: read the buildup. Keep the feet moving. Lose slowly enough to learn something.

Vell uncapped the pen. "Begin."

Feryn's shoulder loosened, a small companionable roll, and his lead foot slid back half a step as if he meant to wait and watch — and the world leaned. That fast. No gathering Cael could see, no warning in the air, just weight arriving from everywhere in front of him at once, a hand the size of the yard closing. His guard folded inward like wet paper. The pressure took him off his feet, drove him flat, and the packed dirt slammed the air out of his chest in one white burst.

He lay in the churned scuff of his own landing, mouth working, lungs refusing.

The crowd made a sound that had sympathy in it. That was worse than jeering.

Breathe. His ribs unlocked on the third try and the air came back in, thin and expensive. He rolled to a knee, then to his feet, and made himself look at Feryn while he did it, because Corvane's first page said the fight tells you everything between exchanges if you watch.

Feryn had not moved from where he released. He stood easy, weight centered, watching Cael get up with the pen-stroke patience of a man adding a line to a sum. The shoulder-roll. The half-step. Cael turned them over while his breath rebuilt. Neither had looked like anything. That meant they were for hiding something — and the only thing worth hiding, in a Path built on buildup, was the buildup.

Cael set his guard again two strides inside the western chalk, crowd murmur at his back, nowhere behind him but feet and wall. Feryn came on at a walk, unhurried, closing the circle's middle ground, and Cael watched not the shoulder and not the feet but the whole frame at once, the way Corvane had said to — tension gathers somewhere, always, the body cannot borrow force from nothing — and there. Under the loose roll of the shoulder, a stillness through Feryn's back, a drawn-bow quality his easy walk was built to bury. Half a second. Cael took the half-second and spent all of it: he could not get out of the wave's whole width with the chalk at his heels, so he angled — guard turned oblique, lead forearm braced across the line the force would travel, body bladed thin behind it.

The release came down like a river changing its mind. It caught the angled forearm and sheared along it instead of through him, wrenching the arm back into his own ribs, and his back foot skidded to the chalk line and held. Held. He stood at the end of it, arm ringing from wrist to elbow, a deep bone-ache blooming in the forearm that would still be there tomorrow. But standing.

The crowd noise rose and broke into pieces of itself.

Feryn stopped. Something changed in his face — not alarm, nothing so large — a revision. The cataloguing look sharpened into attention, and he reset his feet a width narrower, and Cael understood with a cold, clarifying drop of the stomach that everything before this moment had been Feryn at tour-weight, and the fight proper started now.

They circled. Cael gave ground along the curve of the chalk, south, keeping the angle open, breath still shorter than he wanted, ribs complaining on the deep pulls, forearm throbbing where it hung in guard. Feryn came on with the same downhill economy but the disguise ran deeper this time, the shoulder-roll gone, the half-step folded into ordinary walking, and Cael watched the frame, watched the frame, watched the frame — and caught it early. Earlier than the last time. The drawn-bow stillness, gathering under the collarbone, a full breath before release instead of half — and his body answered before he did, hip dropping, weight pouring sideways into the loose churned dirt his own first fall had ploughed, and the dirt gave exactly enough, and something in his legs that he had not summoned and could not have summoned lifted him through the slide the way a gust lifts paper, the wind-thing arriving unasked at the moment pressure squeezed it out of him — and the wave broke over the place where he had just been standing.

Dust jumped off the vacated ground in a flat ring and drifted into the first row.

The yard went silent, then went loud.

Cael came out of the slide upright, three strides south of his own ghost, forearm alight, ribs alight, and Feryn turned to face him — turned all the way, squared, no catalogue left anywhere in his eyes — and gave him the flat, level regard one fighter gives another when the arithmetic has been thrown out. Whatever this match had been arranged as, it had stopped being that.

Feryn came forward. No walk this time.

The buildup started before his second stride and it did not gather the way the others gathered — it compressed, the drawn-bow stillness and the loosing crushed together into almost one motion, and Cael read it, he read it clean, and the reading did not matter because the release was already arriving inside his half-second. He got the hip drop started. The slide began. And Feryn was somehow at the end of it already, inside the dust of it, and a hand closed on Cael's shoulder like a shutting door.

The hand did not stop his momentum. It steered it. All of Cael's sideways speed swung around the grip-point and became downward speed, his own evasion folded into the throw, the ground trading places with the sky.

And the hand stayed.

Longer than the throw needed — the redirect had finished, his fall had been decided, and still Feryn's grip rode his shoulder down through it, a full held beat, two, pressure flowing through the palm print like water finding a channel, and some part of Cael that was not thinking about the ground noticed the wrongness of it, filed it, lost it —

The yard hit him everywhere at once.

He heard the crowd from a long way off. His arms answered slowly, half-message. He got an elbow under himself and the elbow said no, and the dirt was cool against his cheek, and his body reported in from a dozen precincts at once — ribs, forearm, hip, the printed ache of a hand on his shoulder — none of it sharp, all of it total.

"Match to Feryn." Vell's voice, even and carrying, pen already moving. "Recorded."

The call landed while Cael's arms were still negotiating. He let his forehead rest on the dirt for one breath and did not argue, because there was nothing to argue. He could not stand inside the count. The count existed for exactly this. A referee who waited on him would have been doing him a kindness, and kindness was the one thing this yard had never once recorded in a ledger.

By the time he got to a knee — slowly, in stages, like furniture being moved — Feryn had crossed to Vell's stool and confirmed the entry and collected nothing yet, and then he came back. He crouched at the chalk's inner edge, forearms on his knees, and looked at Cael from level height for the first time all evening.

"Who taught you to read buildup?" he said.

Cael's mouth took a moment to find itself. "Someone patient."

"Not patient enough to finish." Feryn said it without heat, a professional noting a professional thing. "You read the third one before I'd have read it. Then you stood there being right." He rose, unhurried. "Being right is a place to start from, not a place to stand."

He looked down a moment longer — at Cael, at the shoulder his hand had ridden, Cael could not have said which — and something unresolved crossed his face and left it. Then he nodded once, the small equal nod that Bronze did not owe unranked, and walked to collect his purse.

Cael sat back on his heels in the middle of the circle and breathed, ribs charging him for it, and let the crowd's noise wash past without sorting it. His forearm pulsed. His shoulder — the shoulder — hummed under the bruise with a faint, settling warmth that did not feel like a bruise, like a coin dropped into a well still turning on its way down. Bruises did strange things. He filed it with the other strange things and reached for the thought that mattered instead, the one with edges:

He had read it. Three weeks of paper and the read had worked, live, twice — and reading had lost anyway, because between the read and the body stood a distance he had not trained, and Feryn had simply been faster than the distance. That could be measured. That could be closed.

Vell was still writing. Whatever he was recording took more lines than a loss usually took.

Cael got one foot flat, then the other, and stood in stages, and nobody laughed, and that — in this yard, in front of these particular faces — was its own kind of entry in its own kind of ledger.

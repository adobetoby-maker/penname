# Chapter Nine

Toren started keeping a tally on the fourth day of it, on the back of his father's paper, under the column of days.

The paper had bridge tolls on the front in Garrick's blocky hand, and on the back, in the same hand, a column of numbers counting down to Meridian that stopped at eighteen because the man who had been keeping it had stopped. Toren had taken it out of the coat at the road cut and had not been able to make himself throw it away, and somewhere north of Hobb's Wall, he had begun writing in it, in his own smaller, worse hand, underneath.

What he wrote now was not days. It was a five-bar gate.

Each mark was a reason.

*Because you're strong enough to get away with it on a two-foot wall and you won't be on a five-foot one.* That was the first one, at the wall, on the morning after the fire, and Toren had written it down that night because he had honestly believed it was going to be the only one.

*Because the hedge doesn't stop anything, it just stops you seeing what's coming through it, and a thing that stops you seeing is worse than nothing at all — nothing at all you'll watch.*

*Because if you're going to lie to me about how tired you are, lie about something I can't check. I can see your feet.*

*Because Milo calls where it is and you call what we're doing about it, and those are two different jobs, and if one voice tries to do both of them, the second one gets thin.*

Four marks in four days. Then, on the fifth day, six marks before noon, and Toren stopped writing them down because there was no longer any point in keeping a tally of the weather.

He did not tell anybody he had been doing it. He was not sure why. It felt, obscurely, like the sort of thing you did not show people, like counting the days.

---

The other change was that Dessa stopped being someone the drill happened around.

It began as a practical matter — she could not drill, she was sitting on the bank anyway, and Rook, on the fifth morning, walked up the bank and said, "You've got the best view in the field. Tell me what Milo's doing with his feet."

"Which foot?"

"Either. Both. Tell me what you see and don't tell me what you think it means."

She told him. She was very good at it, and got better within about an hour. By the afternoon Rook had stopped asking and had simply started running the drill with his back to half of it, and twice Toren heard the man say "Say again?" up the bank, and heard Dessa say it again, and watched him change the drill on the strength of it.

Wyck did not like that.

He did not say he did not like it. What he did was start putting his shoulder into the wall crossings a little harder than the drill needed, and going quiet for stretches, and answering Dessa's calls with "Got it," flat, without turning his head, in a way that was correct in every particular and pleasant in none.

"He's not angry at me," Dessa said, on the bank, when Toren brought it up.

"He's making a decent job of it."

"He's angry that she's got a job," said Dessa, "and I've got a job, and Milo's got a job, and you've got a job, and it's the middle, and what he's got is *being strong*, which isn't a job, it's a fact, and he's had it his whole life, and nobody's ever once had to teach him anything about it." She shrugged. "He's fifteen, and he's watching the rest of us learn things. I'd be unbearable."

---

On the sixth day Wyck asked a question.

They had come down out of the field systems into a shallow valley with a canal in it — a real one, cut straight, forty feet across, the water black and still and about as deep as a man, with a towpath on the near side that made the best road they had walked in a week. Rook put them on the towpath and drilled them on it because it was twelve feet wide with water on one side, which meant it was a corridor, and a corridor was a thing they had not had.

He was in the middle of taking Toren's line apart for the third time when Wyck said, "Why does it matter which end I'm on?"

He said it the way he said things now, which was flatly, and with the air of a man who has decided in advance not to get an answer and is asking anyway in order to prove something to himself.

Rook stopped.

"Say the whole question."

"You keep putting me on the water end." Wyck's chin came up slightly. "Every time. Canal side, water at my back, and I keep asking myself why, and the only answer I've got is that the water end's where you put the one you're least worried about losing."

Milo said, "*Wyck.*"

"I'm asking him a question."

"Fair question," said Rook.

He walked back down the towpath and stood at the water end and looked at it for a second.

"Two reasons and you're not going to like the second one," he said. "First: a man can only be flanked from the sides he's got. On this path you've got one. That's the cheapest thing I can give anybody, and I give it to whoever's holding longest, and on a line that's you, because you plant and you don't move, and you've done it since the tailgate in the cut, and nobody taught you that, and I haven't got the first idea where you got it.

"Second: you're the only one of the four who'll go in."

Wyck looked at him.

"If it takes you off the path, you'll come up swinging, and if it takes Milo off the path, Milo will drown, because he's nine stone and can't swim and he'd have a light in his hands. So I put the end that touches the water next to the one who can survive touching it. That's all of it. That's the entire reasoning, and it took me about a second and a half, and I've been doing it for six days."

He went back up the line.

"And it's not a compliment," he said over his shoulder. "It's an inventory. I do it with the mule."

Wyck stood at the water end of the towpath with his mouth slightly open.

"Right," he said.

He was quiet for the rest of the morning, but it was a different quiet, and Toren — who had been doing very little all week except reading that particular boy for weather — could not work out whether it was better or worse.

---

The thing with the Ember happened on the seventh day, and it was, in the end, completely undramatic, which Toren resented for about a year afterward.

Rook had spent three days stripping the problem down.

"You've proved holding it harder puts it out. Good. Now here's the next bit, and you're going to hate it: stop holding it at all."

"Then it goes out."

"Then it goes out. Yes." Rook picked a stone off the towpath and dropped it in the canal. "Both your answers are wrong, which means the question's wrong. You've been asking *how do I keep it*. Nobody keeps it. It isn't a thing you've got hold of that you can lose your grip on." He looked at Toren. "Where were you looking when it went out in the yard?"

"At Milo."

"No, you weren't. Milo was on the ground with a husk on him, and you were looking at *your hand*." He said it without any particular emphasis. "You looked down to check that you still had it, and that was the instant. I watched you do it. You've done it eleven times since, and I've counted every one of them."

Toren did not say anything.

"So you're going to spend today doing something else with the front of your head," said Rook, "and I don't care what. Carry something heavy. Do sums. Talk. It won't work and then one time it will and you won't know why, and afterward you'll want the reason and I'll give you what I've got, which is less than you'd like."

What Toren did that day was carry a sack of milled corn up and down four hundred yards of towpath with the spike out in his right hand and Milo's calls coming at him from behind, and count them out loud.

It was the most stupid thing he had ever done in his life. The sack weighed about forty pounds and the towpath was uneven and the spike stuck out in front of him like a poorly held candle and Milo, who had been given free rein for the first time since Sallow Creek, was calling positions for imaginary husks with escalating creative enthusiasm.

"Two left — one's at nine feet — three left, one's *behind* you—"

"There's nothing behind me."

"Behind you at four feet and it's enormous—"

"Milo."

"Call it, Tor."

"*Left three, one at nine feet, one behind at four* — this is idiotic —"

"Left *two* now, one's gone down the bank, left two—"

"Left two, one down the bank—"

Rook hit him from the side with a shoulder and put him flat on the towpath with the sack on top of him.

Toren went down with the whole weight of it across his chest and his face in the gravel, and he came up spitting and furious with the sack in one arm and his other hand out.

The spike was still there.

He looked at it. He had not looked at it. It had been in his fist through the shoulder and the fall and the gravel, and it was still there, still ugly, still crooked, and it was warm.

That was the part that stopped him.

It had been cool in his father's hands and cool in the coals at Sallow Creek and cool in a weigh-yard in a woman's opinion and cool against his leg through every mile of road since, cool the way the inside of a well is cool, and it had been cool four seconds ago.

It was warm now, the way a cup is warm through a glove. It went on being warm.

"Oh," Toren said.

He sat down on the towpath with a sack of corn across his knees and held out his own hand and looked at it. The light off the canal came up under the spike, and it was the least impressive thing anybody had ever seen, and he could feel it in his palm like something breathing.

Rook crouched down in front of him.

"That's Ember," he said.

"That's—"

"That's the rank. That's the word people will use about you now, and they'll be right. Kindled's a door standing open. Ember is the first thing that stays lit when you stop looking at it." He held out a hand, and Toren, without thinking, put the spike out to him, and Rook pushed it aside impatiently and got hold of his wrist instead, two fingers on the inside of it, the way he had done with Senna at the cut. He held it for three seconds and let go. "Yes. All right."

"What did I do?"

"You stopped auditing it."

"That's not —"

"I said you wouldn't like it." Rook stood up. "You spent four days trying to find out where the power *is*, and the answer's the same as it was in the field, and it'll be the same in a year, and every time somebody hands it to you in words, you'll nod and it won't take. So we do it the other way round. You'll do it first and get told after." He looked down. "It went warm."

"Yes."

"Remember that it did. That's the whole of what I'm asking."

Then he turned round and said, in a completely different voice, "Wyck. Bring the bar. He's going to get up and hold that while you try to take it off him, and it's going to go out, and he's going to be intolerable about it for a week."

---

It went out eleven times that afternoon and came back twelve, and by evening Toren could hold it through a shove, through a fall, and through being shouted at, and could not hold it through being surprised from behind, and Rook said that was correct and that it would be correct for about two months.

At the fire, Milo said, "So what's everybody, then?"

"Don't," said Wyck.

"No, properly. If we're saying the words now. What is everybody."

Rook did not look up from the flask. "You're all Kindled except him, and he went up this afternoon."

"And Dessa?"

There was a short silence of the kind Milo walked into about twice a week.

"Kindled," said Dessa, from the cart step. "Say it, it's not a swear word."

"But you—"

"I did a thing for four seconds once, and I haven't been able to do it since," said Dessa. "I've sat on six banks. I'm Kindled. When I'm something else, you'll know because I'll be insufferable about it, like Toren's grandfather."

"He was *dreadful*," said Senna, from inside the cart, sounding perfectly delighted.

Wyck said, "And Kindled's the bottom."

"Kindled's the door," said Rook.

"Right," said Wyck. "The bottom."

He said it without heat, and he got up and went to check the hobble on the mule, which did not need checking, and Toren sat on the other side of the fire with a warm stone in his coat pocket and understood two things.

The first was that the thing in his pocket had gone warm today and that it was going to be warm tomorrow.

The second was that he was not going to be able to enjoy it in front of Wyck Tanner, and that he had about a week before that stopped being a small problem.

---

Rook put the flask away and stood up, and the way he did it made everybody look.

"We're going off the road tomorrow," he said. "West, four miles, to an orchard on a slope above a creek. I've been there twice. There'll be something in it."

"Something," said Dessa.

"There's always something in an orchard." He put another stick on the fire, which he did not usually bother to do. "Sit down, all of you. I'm going to tell you what's in it, and what it does, and what it looks like an instant before it does it, and I'm going to do it now, tonight, while you're warm and nobody's bleeding."

Nobody moved for a second.

Then Milo sat down, and Wyck sat down, and Dessa got herself off the cart step and along to the fire with the stick, and Toren sat where he was with his hand on a warm stone in his coat pocket and did not say the thing he was thinking, which was that thirteen days ago on a bridge in the fog this man had told them, with his face not moving, that he had kept a tell back on purpose and that he would do it again and that they were not going to like most of the trades he made.

Rook began to talk.

Toren listened to all of it and wrote none of it down, and lay awake for a while afterward turning the whole day over — the sack, the towpath, the warm stone — and got nowhere near the bottom of it, and fell asleep instead.

# Chapter Ten

What Rook had told them, at the fire, the night before, with a stick in his hand and all four of them sitting down and nobody bleeding, was this:

"Stilts, is what I call them. They stand about as high as me, and about nine-tenths of it is leg. Two legs, jointed backwards, longer than they've any business being. A body slung between them, no bigger than a dog. A head you won't be able to find, and won't need to. Colour of wet ash, same as the hounds, same as everything.

"Husks come at you. Hounds choose a side, and commit, and can't take it back. Learn both of those. They're good. They'll keep you alive for years.

"These do neither. I want you to understand why, because the why is the only part you'll be able to use. There's nothing in them that has to gather itself up. A dog crouches. A man leans. Anything that has to build speed has to start building it somewhere. That somewhere is the thing I've spent two weeks teaching you to watch for. These don't build it. Stopped, and going flat out, are the same state to them. There is no instant in between. If you wait to see it start, it has finished.

"So don't watch them. Watch the *lines*.

"They go in straight lines, and they can't bend one once they're in it. They have to stop to choose again. When they stop, they're worth nothing. That's the whole of your chance. The orchard's planted in rows across the slope. A row is a line. The ditch at the bottom of the orchard is a line. The gap where the fence is down is a line. Anywhere you can see a long way, something can come the whole way in no time at all; anywhere there's a tree trunk between you and it, it has to stop first.

"Stand where the trees are. Never stand at the end of a row. If you find yourself looking down a long clear stretch of anything, you are already in the wrong place. You should move before you finish the thought."

Then he had said, "Say it back," and made them, one at a time, all four, until Milo could do it in his sleep. Wyck had done it twice, in a voice of enormous patience.

Toren had gone to sleep with his hand on a warm stone, thinking that this was the easiest thing anyone had ever asked him to remember.

---

They saw the hedge thing on the track out, about two miles in, and it did Toren more damage than anything else that happened that day.

It was in the thorn hedge on the north side of the farm track, sixty feet off: a low grey shape that came out of the scrub at a lope, and stopped.

Toren's whole spine went cold, and his mouth was already moving.

He never afterward got the order of it right, not for Dessa and not for anybody. There was no seeing the thing and then deciding about it. There was the grey shape coming out of the scrub and stopping, and then a cold that started at the back of his neck and went the whole length of him at once, the way it goes when somebody lays a wet rope along your spine. Riding in the middle of the cold, arriving with it and not behind it, was a direction. Right. Not a guess at right. Not a reckoning he could have shown anybody the working of. Right, the way you know which of your own hands is your right hand, without having to look at either of them.

Where it came from, he had no idea. Lying flat in a cart in the dark, some time later, he decided it came up out of somewhere near the place the warmth came up out of, or near enough. He never got any closer to it than that in his life.

"*Right*," he said. "One right, in the hedge, it's gone still, it's choosing — it's going to come and it's coming right, everybody right—"

They turned right. Wyck planted. Milo got the call out clean behind him. Rook stepped out onto the verge, and stood on it, with his hands down, and his weight back on his right heel, and his chin a little lowered, doing nothing whatever. The verge-hound came out of the thorn at them in a flat silent rush, at the exact angle Toren had called, and met four people who were already facing it, and a man who took its front leg off at the shoulder in one movement.

Toren got three pieces of that movement. He did not get the rest of it, then or ever, and the three he got did not arrive in the order they had happened in.

He got the boot. The right one, the one the weight had been on, turning a quarter of the way round in the verge grass, screwing down into it, and the weight coming off it, and forward.

He got the hand. It had been empty at Rook's side for the whole of the four seconds Toren had been looking at it, hanging. Then it was up, and it was not empty, and there was no instant available anywhere between the two states for it to have got that way in.

He got the sound, and the sound was the worst of it, because it was such a small one: a single short scrape, like a spade going into wet gravel, over before he had finished hearing it begin.

After that the hound was going past them with its whole front end folding under it, and the shoulder was open and grey and pouring. The leg was lying in the grass a good way behind where the leg had been, and the thing put its head into the track, and slid, and stopped, and did not get up.

And there was a second one behind it that broke off before it had committed, and went away along the hedge line, and did not come back.

What Toren felt first was not relief, and was not gratitude. It was a small sick lurch of wrongness, the exact lurch of putting your foot down on a stair that is not there. He had been carrying a picture of Rook fighting since the road cut, built out of a sound coming down a rock face and things Milo said at night, and in the picture there was a man moving very fast. This had not been fast. Fast has a beginning; he had spent two weeks being taught to watch for beginnings. This had been too *clean* — a whole event of a size that ought to have cost two seconds and some shouting, paid for instead with one quarter-turn of a boot heel and one scrape, nothing spare in it anywhere, nothing wasted, nothing even hurried. Some part of Toren's head went on, for several steps, insisting that something had been left out of it, and would have to be paid for later.

Thirty seconds. Nobody touched.

"Well," said Dessa, from the cart.

Milo was laughing in the high way he had when he had been frightened. "He *called* it. Tor called it before it moved."

"He called it right," said Rook.

And he did not say anything else.

Four words, with no instruction in them and nothing after them, and they went into Toren like drink on an empty stomach. Because what they meant was that the cold had been information. Not nerves. Not a boy's stomach turning over at a grey shape in a hedge, and his mouth running ahead of him, and getting lucky. A reading — the same class of thing as Milo's positions and Dessa's feet, a thing that came in, and could be said out loud, and could be *right*. And a thing that could be right could be trusted, and a thing that could be trusted did not have to be checked. Not checking was faster. Faster was the whole of the job.

And Toren walked the next two miles with his ears ringing, and his chest about four inches further out than it had been. Every step of those two miles laid down a little more of the thing that was going to cost them at four o'clock.

---

The orchard was on the south slope above the creek, and it had not been picked in sixty years.

It ran about two hundred yards across the hill, and seven rows down it, old trees grown together over their own windfall, and the rows were as straight as the day somebody had pegged them out. Along the bottom, between the lowest row and the creek bank, ran a stone-lined ditch, four feet deep, and as straight as the rows. At the western end stood the press-house: one room of dressed stone, with the roof fallen in and one wall standing to eight feet, a doorway in it, and three worn steps going up to the doorway from the orchard floor.

"Cart there," said Rook, pointing at the press-house. "Against the wall, mule tied short. Dessa, up on the steps with your grandmother — up on the top step, so you can see over the rows. You call what you see, and don't call what you think it means."

"Yes."

"Senna, you stay in that cart whatever happens, and you keep your head below the boards."

"I've been in this cart since Sallow Creek," said Senna. "It's the only thing in my life I'm actually good at."

"Milo, light in your hands the whole time, and you keep the pair of them behind you." He turned. "Wyck, Toren. Third row down, the pair of you, at the thick end where the trunks are close. Not the fourth. The fourth's got the fence gap at the end of it."

"Where are you?" said Toren.

"Where I need to be."

---

There were four of them in the orchard, which was what Rook had said there would be.

The first one Toren saw was standing three rows down and eighty feet along, between two trees, and it was so still, and so exactly the colour of the grey trunks, that he looked straight through it twice.

Then it was thirty feet closer.

There was no motion between the two positions. Toren's eye did not record a journey; it recorded one thing, and then another thing. The only evidence that any travel had occurred was the sound, which came half an instant late, and which Toren knew, and which turned his stomach over: a series of hard flat cracks, spaced, like a stone skipping across water.

He had heard it exactly once before, coming down a rock face forty feet above a road, out of a man he did not yet know.

"*Third row, two of them, third row!*" — Dessa, from the steps, clear and hard.

"Two in the row," Milo called, "one at forty, one at sixty—"

"Two in the row, one at forty," Toren said after him, and got the spike out, and it came out warm.

Then the row discipline worked, and it worked so well that it was almost boring.

That was the shape of the next four minutes. A Stilt at the end of a row was a straight line, and a straight line was a killing thing, so they did not stand in rows. They stood *across* them, in the thick of the trunks where the old trees had grown into each other. Every time one of the things wanted to reach them, it had to stop out in the open to choose a new line, and the instant it stopped, it was a two-legged thing standing on the grass, doing nothing.

Wyck killed the first one with the bar across the knee of its left leg, while it stood picking its way through the geometry, and the whole creature went down like a stepladder coming apart.

Toren killed the second one poorly, and in three goes, and would have been in trouble if Rook had not come through the row behind him, with no warning at all, and taken its other leg off.

"Trunks," Rook said, and was gone.

"I *am* on the trunks—"

"You're a foot off them."

The third one broke off, and went up the slope into the windbreak, and did not come back down.

Toren stood in the third row with his heart going and a warm spike in his fist, and thought: *we're doing it. We're actually doing it.*

---

Dessa said, "*Ditch.*"

She said it in a completely different voice from the one she had been using, and it went across the orchard like a thrown stone.

"Ditch — the ditch, the ditch, there's things in the *ditch* — three — four, four in the ditch and they're coming up it, they're coming *west* —"

West was the press-house.

West was the cart.

Toren turned and looked down the slope, and could see nothing at all, because the ditch was four feet deep, and stone-lined, and dead straight, and anything in it was invisible from the third row, and was travelling along the cleanest line on that entire hillside.

He had heard Rook say *the ditch at the bottom of the orchard is a line*. He had said it back, out loud, at a fire, the night before. He had understood it perfectly, and it had gone into him next to nine other things, and he had spent the last four minutes thinking about rows.

"*Down!*" Rook shouted, from somewhere up the slope. "Everyone west — press-house — *now*, go, go—"

They went. Running downhill across old orchard rows with windfall under the grass is not running, it is a controlled fall with interruptions, and Toren went over twice, and kept the spike both times, and did not look at his hand.

The Stilts came out of the ditch at the western end, where the stone gave out, and the bank shelved, and they came out one behind another. The ground between the mouth of the ditch and the press-house steps was the one piece of open mown-flat grass in the whole orchard.

The last Toren had seen of Rook, the man had been forty yards up the slope and two rows over, standing side-on between two trees, with one hand flat on a trunk and his head turned downhill, not moving at all.

Rook got there first by an enormous margin. Toren heard the struts fire twice, and did not see the man cross any of it.

He could not have seen it. He was running downhill over windfall with his eyes on the ground a yard in front of his own boots, because that was where his eyes had to be. So what he had was the sound, and the sound was two hard flat cracks, with the second one about as far behind the first as it takes to say a short word, and under each of them, for about as long as it takes to notice a thing and lose it again, something that was not quite a hum. The first crack came from behind him and above him. The second came from a long way in front of him and below. In between there was nothing — no body going past him, no grass noise, no draught, no breath, none of the ordinary evidence that a grown man has been through the forty yards you are standing in the middle of.

When he got his eyes up off the ground at the bottom of the slope, which took about a second and a half, the world had been rearranged.

Rook was standing on the flat mown grass in front of the press-house steps, with his back to the cart. Not arriving. Not braking, not going down on one knee, not taking two skidding steps to kill the speed of anything — standing, feet already set, weight already down, coat already hanging straight, in the posture of a man who had been in that spot for half a minute, and was waiting on the rest of them.

A second and a half. Toren had time, running, to have that in his head, and to get nothing whatever out of it.

"Line!" Rook shouted. "On me — *across* the open, not along it —"

They made a line eleven feet in front of the press-house steps, with the open grass in front of them, and the fallen wall at their backs, and a trodden windfall floor under their feet. For about ninety seconds it was the best thing the four of them had ever done together.

Milo had the light up over his head, and did not drop it. Dessa called from the top step with the whole slope under her eye, and got every single one right. Wyck planted at the left end, because the left end was where the mouth of the ditch was, and he did not have to be told. Toren held the middle, and said everything Milo said one beat behind him, louder, and twice Rook did what the middle said.

They killed two more on the open grass. Rook took one in the air, which Toren had not known was possible.

It came up out of the mouth of the ditch already going flat out, and it did not come at the line. It went across the front of them, left to right, at about the height of a man's chest, on a line that would have taken it round the end of the line altogether, and up onto the steps.

Rook was at the right-hand end of the open grass, eight or ten feet clear of Milo, and he was not facing the ditch. He was facing along the run, square to a piece of empty air the thing had not got to yet, and he had been standing like that, doing nothing, since before it came out. Toren did not understand for a long time that the standing was the whole of the work, and everything after it was only spending.

Then Rook's left boot went out and forward, and took all of him.

There was no crouch. Toren was watching for one — two weeks of being taught that anything which has to build speed has to start building it somewhere — and he was looking straight at the place where the gathering ought to have been, and there was no gathering. The man put the foot down where the foot needed to be, and let his whole weight arrive on top of it. The knee bent about a hand's width, and stopped bending.

The strut fired. It was a shorter sound than the ones on the slope had been, clipped off short at the end, a single flat crack with the ground in it.

And Rook came off the grass.

Something went over sideways in Toren's chest. It was not admiration; there was no room in the afternoon for admiration. It was the flat refusal of a boy who had been raised on a hill farm, and knew, the way you know which way down is, that a man who leaves the ground is a man who is falling. This one was not falling. He was going up, and he was going along, and he was doing it on purpose, and for half a second Toren's head simply declined the whole business, the way a mule declines a plank bridge.

Then he went back to looking, because it was still happening, and because — this was the part he could never afterward make anybody understand — it was slow.

That was the thing. On the ground, the man was a noise and a change of position. In the air he was a man. Ordinary. Ugly, even: he had gone up at an angle, and half turned away from the run, so that his right shoulder led, his right arm folded in tight against his ribs, his left arm out and trailing low behind him for balance, his legs apart and doing nothing at all. He hung out there on a curve Toren could see the whole of, the way anything hangs that has been thrown, with his coat come open, and the hem of it lifted, and his face not doing anything. And there was time. Real time, the ordinary kind, enough of it to see all of that, and to know, while it was going on, that he was seeing it.

Toren worked out the why of it a long while afterward, lying awake, and never checked it with anybody: you can only push against what is under you, and in the air there is nothing under you. Whatever the struts were, they were finished at the boot. The rest had been arithmetic, and the arithmetic had been done standing still on the grass, facing the wrong way, before the thing was out of the ditch.

The Stilt did not see him. It could not have. It had chosen its line, and it could not bend it, and the line ran along the grass, and Rook was above the grass.

They met about four feet up.

Rook's right arm came out from his ribs at the top of the curve, and the light came out with it, and went out in front of it. Toren saw the light get there first. He saw it run out past the fist, and keep on running, further than any arm's worth of reach, and find its edges in the last foot of it. The edges arrived on the creature a clear instant before the hand would have.

It took the thing across the back of the body, where the legs were slung into it.

There was no noise of the kind he was braced for. There was one dry snap, like a fence rail going, and then a long tearing hiss as the ash let go. The two long legs ran on without the body for six or seven feet, still in the line they had chosen, having nothing left in them to choose again with, and then folded up, and came down, and slid, and were only legs.

Rook came down on the grass with all of it under him — both boots, then one hand flat — and the ash went over him and past him in a grey sheet. He got up out of it the way a man gets up off a low wall, moving before he was upright, already turned to the mouth of the ditch, already counting what was left.

Toren became aware that his own mouth was open, and shut it.

And then the fourth one stopped at the mouth of the ditch, forty feet out, side-on, with its body down low between the long legs.

It stood absolutely still.

Toren's whole spine went cold — and the cold arrived with a name on it, and the name was a gift, and it was the wrong gift, and it was his.

"*Right!*" he shouted. "It's chosen — it's going right, it's committed right, turn right, everybody *right*—"

Milo said, "It hasn't—"

"It's the wind-up, Milo, it's *going*—"

And the line turned right, because Toren was in the middle, and the middle was where the calls came from.

The thing at the mouth of the ditch was not winding up. It was not doing anything. It had stopped in the open to choose a line, exactly as Rook had said it would. The line it chose was the one the four of them had just opened for it by turning, and there was no instant between the choosing and the arriving.

It came through the left end of the line at the speed of a dropped stone, and it did not touch anybody, because there was nobody there any more. Behind it, the mouth of the ditch put up another one.

Two of them, inside the line, between the line and the press-house steps, and on the steps there was a boy with a light in his hands, a girl who could not run, and an old woman in a cart.

---

Toren did not decide anything.

That was what he told Dessa afterward, and it was true, and it was not the whole truth. He did not decide; but for four days, he had been carrying around a thing he had been told to carry — *stop auditing it* — and for two weeks, he had been carrying a thing he had watched: a girl on the other side of a fire with a stone going warm in her hands, saying *it's not in the stone*.

What he did was stop asking.

He did not ask the Ember for anything. He did not hold it harder. He did not look at his hand.

Every other time in his life, asking had been a reaching. His attention went down onto the place where the weight sat, and he got hold of the neck of it, and he tipped out a measured amount, carefully, the way you pour from a sack you have to carry the rest of the day. He had got good at that. He had got good at it, the way a man gets good at a job done with his hands tied.

What he did at the mouth of the ditch was let go of the neck.

That was all of it, and it took no time, and it was not a decision. He simply stopped measuring. He took the front of his head off the place where the weight sat — off the checking, off the counting, off the whole long habit of making sure there was still something there to spend — and put all of it out in front of him instead, on the nearer of the two, and on the boy with the light on the steps behind it.

He turned and went after the nearer of the two, with the whole of himself out in front of him and nothing whatsoever held back. Somewhere behind his own eyes, a door that had been standing an inch open since a road cut came the rest of the way open, and everything behind it came through at once.

The spike changed.

He felt it go. It went out of the slab first, at the base of his fist, a pull like a rope coming taut through a ring. Then it ran out of his fist in a single clean movement, thinner and half again as long, and not ugly at all.

He saw it make itself. It was not there and then bright; it was bright before it was anything — a line of white going out past his knuckles ahead of any edge at all, running out into the air the way water runs down a dry channel, finding the length first and the shape after. Then the edges came, and they came fast, arriving along it from the fist outward, the way frost goes across a puddle. They came up true and thin and matched, one and then the other. The blunt hooked end that had been on the thing since the road cut arrived last of all, and arrived straight.

It committed. He felt the moment it decided what it was going to be, the way you feel the last of a knot go when you pull it.

And the whole of his forearm went to light — not a glove of it, not a shine on the skin. It came back up out of the stone into his hand, and past his wrist, and stopped at the elbow, and he could feel it in the bone, and the arm was not holding the weapon any more. The arm was part of it.

And the world went very simple. He put it through the first Stilt lengthwise, and took both legs off the second one on the backswing, and neither of them touched the ground before he had turned for a third.

There was no third.

There was nothing at all. The bottom came out of him the way the bottom comes out of a wet sack.

What he knew about running low was the end of a long drill — a thinning, a going-slack, the sort of tiredness a man can feel coming from a long way off, and can stop by stopping. It had nothing to do with this. This did not come. It went: all of it, at once, downward and outward, out of the breastbone, and out of the belly, and out through the backs of his knees. It emptied so completely that on the way down he could feel the exact size of what had been in him by the size of the hole it left. The hole was a great deal bigger than he had ever supposed the thing was.

He was on his knees. He had not been aware of any part of that. The spike was gone — not withdrawn, not put away, simply not there — and the grey slab in his fist was cold, cold the way the inside of a well is cold, cold the way it had been on every morning of his life before this one. His arms would not lift, and his ears were full of a high flat sound, and he could not make his head come up off his chest.

The place low in him where the weight had sat was not empty the way a cup is empty. It was empty the way a house is empty. He put his attention down onto it anyway, because he could not stop himself, because it was the oldest habit he had — checking, auditing, making sure — and there was nothing down there to check.

*Get up*, he thought.

He watched his own hand lie in the grass in front of him, and do nothing.

*Get up. Get up. Left end. There's nothing on the left end.*

---

There was something on the left end.

Wyck Tanner stepped across the gap where Toren should have been, which meant standing at the only place on that whole hillside where the mouth of the ditch had a clean straight run at the press-house steps. He set his feet, and he did not move.

Toren saw it from the ground, four feet away, with his cheek in the windfall.

He saw the feet get set, which took about as long as putting a bucket down: the left boot out and turned a little off square, the right boot back, and the heel of the right boot working itself twice into the windfall until there was something under it. He saw the shoulders come round. He saw the bar come up across the body, and stay there, at the angle a man holds a bar when he has not decided yet which side of him it is going to be wanted on.

Nothing else about Wyck Tanner did anything at all. He did not sink at the hips. He did not fill his chest. He did not roll a shoulder forward, or turn side-on, or take the half-step back that every man takes, because there was nothing in him that had to be got ready.

There was nothing to see. That was the whole of it. Toren was four feet away, at ground level, looking straight along the line of those boots, and there was nothing whatever to see.

The Stilt came out of the mouth of the ditch at the speed those things came out of everything, and it hit Wyck square, and there was a sound like a barrow going over on a stone floor. Wyck did not go anywhere.

He should have. Nine stone of boy, and a thing that had crossed forty feet in no time at all, and it came off him like water off a gatepost, and Wyck's boots were exactly where Wyck's boots had been.

Things moved. The windfall in front of him jumped, and a spray of it went up between the two of them and came down. His coat went back off his thighs, and came down again. The hair came off his forehead and settled. Everything on that hillside that was small and light did what small light things do when something of that size arrives.

The boots did not move. Not slid. Not turned, not rocked back onto the heels and come forward again — did not move, and Toren was lying at the end of the line of them, and would have seen a quarter-inch. The shoulders did not go back. The head did not snap. The bar stayed at the angle it had been at, which was the part he went over most often in the years after, because a man being hit by anything at all lets go of the angle of the thing in his hands; it is the first thing to go, before his feet, before his face.

And there was no flare. There was no light anywhere near him, nothing came up out of the ground, and nothing went down into it, and nothing about him changed colour or hardened or shone. A thing that could cross forty feet in no time at all hit a boy standing in wet grass, and the boy stayed where he was.

It opened his left forearm from the elbow to the wrist on the way past, and he did not appear to notice, and he swung the bar into its trailing leg as it went, and broke the leg. The thing went down on the steps, and Milo Faust kicked it in the head with both heels, screaming, from above, the way he had kicked one off a cart bed thirteen days ago.

The last one came out of the ditch at Wyck, and did the same thing, and got the same result.

He held the gap for about eleven seconds.

Rook came down the line, and finished all of it, and stopped, and stood in the open grass with his head turning once, twice, and then let his arms down.

The orchard went quiet.

Milo's light was still up.

---

The ash ran down the mown grass and pooled in the shallow places, and Rook let it lie for eleven minutes, which nobody had ever seen him do, because he was working on Wyck's arm.

He worked on it for a long time. He had Dessa hold the lamp — she had come down the steps on the stick without asking anybody, and had got herself onto one knee in the grass, and stayed there — and Milo with two hands where he was put. He had Wyck's arm across his own knee, his Ember against the inside of the elbow, his palm flat over the length of the cut. After a while the bleeding slowed the way water slows when somebody upstream shuts a gate part of the way.

Then he sat back on his heels, and got the flat tin flask out, and looked at what was in it, and drank all of it.

"That's not fixed," he said. "That's slowed. It wants stitching, and I haven't got the thread for a cut that long, so it's going to knit ugly, and it's going to be stiff, and you're going to have it for the rest of your life." He wound the rag. "You'll use that hand tomorrow. Not well. Tomorrow."

"Right," said Wyck.

"Say if you go cold, or the fingers go stupid. Say it out loud when it happens, not an hour after."

"Right."

Rook tied the rag off, and did not let go of the arm.

"Now the other thing," he said.

"What other thing?"

"Two of them hit you at full run, and you're standing where you were standing."

Wyck said nothing.

"How long?" said Rook.

"How long what?"

"How long have you been able to do it."

"I can't *do* anything," said Wyck, and his voice came apart slightly in the middle of it, which was the first time Toren had ever heard that happen. "I haven't got anything. I'm fifteen and nothing, ask the woman with the book, she wrote it down. I just don't —"

"Don't move," said Rook.

"— don't fall over," said Wyck.

"No. You don't."

Rook let go of the arm.

"That's Ember," he said. "That's what it's called, and that's what you are as of about four o'clock this afternoon, and I'd guess you were most of the way there on a tailgate in a road cut thirteen days ago with a bit of rebar, before anybody here had taught you a thing.

"And I'll tell you the part nobody in a settlement will ever tell you, because a settlement can't see it. There are three doors. One of them's a stone somebody hands you at thirteen, and it does a trick, and everyone can watch. One of them's the other business, the wall, the wound, the flask. And one of them is nothing anybody can look at. It doesn't come out of an object, and it doesn't come in from outside. It's a person deciding, over and over, for years, in a lot of small places where it didn't matter, not to be moved — and then one day it isn't a decision any more, it's just true about them, and a thing that crosses forty feet in no time at all comes off them like weather off a wall.

"They'll never put you in a book for it. You can't show it to anybody. You can only be it, and you have been being it since before I met you, and it's the least impressive and most useful thing on this hillside."

Wyck sat on the press-house step with his arm across his knees and his face turned away, and did not say anything at all for a long moment.

Then he said, thickly, "Toren went first."

"Yes."

"He went in front of me. Again."

"Yes," said Rook. "And he's face down in the windfall, and you're sitting up, so let's neither of us pretend that settles anything."

---

Toren could not stand until nearly dark.

That was the part nobody talked about on the way back, and it was the part he hated most. He lay in the orchard grass with the whole of the hillside going on around him — the ash being lifted, the mule being calmed, his grandmother's voice going up and down over the press-house wall in a register of pure fury — and could not get his arms to do anything. When he finally got up, he had to be walked to the cart with Dessa's shoulder under one arm and Milo's under the other, which was, as Milo pointed out, the single most humiliating configuration available to any of the three of them.

They put him in the cart. With the two invalids.

"Well," said Senna. "Look at this."

"Don't."

"*Three* of us."

Rook came to the tailgate, and put two fingers on the inside of Toren's wrist, and held them there a long time.

"Cold," he said.

"It's never been cold."

"It's been cold your whole life until yesterday afternoon, and you'd stopped noticing after one day. That's how fast it happens." He let go. "There's nothing wrong with you. There's nothing in you. It isn't the same thing, and it'll take a week for you to stop confusing them."

"A week?"

"Two days before you can hold the spike. A week before it's worth anything." Rook leaned on the tailgate. "You'll fill again on your own, at the speed a person fills, which is slow and narrow, and it's the only speed there is until somebody shows you otherwise."

"Can you—"

"I could borrow you some," said Rook, "off her, or off him, or off the boy with the light, and then they'd be the ones lying in the cart. That's the entire trade, and it doesn't get better with practice."

"No," said Toren.

"No," Rook agreed.

---

The debrief was that night, in the lee of the windbreak, with the lamp on the cart shaft and all five of them where he could see them.

Toren waited for it with something close to nausea. He knew the shape of this. He had stood on a bridge in the fog eight days ago, and had the shape of it laid over him like a flat stone, and he had deserved it then, and he deserved it a great deal more now. He had spent the whole walk back composing the only sentence he had, which was *I did it again*.

Rook said: "Right things first.

"The girl on the steps called the ditch. She called it four seconds after anything was visible from where she was standing, which is as fast as it could be called by anybody, and she called what she saw, and not what she thought it meant, and if she'd been wrong, we'd have run west for nothing, and I'd have said *good* then too.

"The boy kept a light over his head with his arms shaking for eleven minutes, and called every position he was given, and got none of them wrong. He hasn't got one wrong in fifteen days.

"The one with the bar held a gap on a bad arm, alone, twice, with nobody in support, having stepped into it on his own judgment while the person whose gap it was lay in front of him. Nobody told him to, and nobody had time to.

"And the one in the middle went at two of them, alone, in the two seconds when there was nobody else who could, and killed both. I want all four of you to hear me say that before I say the rest, because the rest is going to take some time."

Nobody moved.

"Now," said Rook. "The call at the mouth of the ditch."

"I know," said Toren.

"Say it."

"I said it was a wind-up. It wasn't. It was standing there." His voice was flat and awful in his own ears. "You told me. You told all of us, last night, sitting down, in words, and I said it back to you twice. *There's no instant in between.* I said that back to you out loud." He pressed his hands into his eyes. "And I did it anyway. And I turned the line, and Wyck's arm—"

"Yes."

"So what was the *point*?" It came out of him louder than he meant it, and Milo flinched. "What was the point of telling us? You said you wouldn't, you said you keep the tell back on purpose, and it costs a leg, and that's the trade, and you'd make it again — and then you told us, and it *still* — so what was it for?"

Rook was quiet for a moment.

"Because you turned the line for two seconds," he said, "instead of four.

"That's all it bought. That's what it was for. On the bridge you were wrong for as long as it took a girl to get three hounds out of a treeline, because you had no other picture in your head to fall back on. Tonight you had one, and you didn't believe it fast enough, and you were wrong for two seconds instead of four. The difference between two and four is why he's got a forearm, and she's got a leg that limps.

"Knowing a thing, and doing it while frightened, are two separate skills. I said that to you on a bridge, and it's still true. What I had wrong—"

He stopped.

He was standing at the edge of the lamplight with his hands in his coat pockets, and he stood there for a second and a half with the sentence unfinished in the air, and Toren, watching him, thought: *he's never done that either.*

"Both of them are trainable," said Rook. "That's all. Go to sleep."

# Chapter 1 — Rated

The bout had ended twenty minutes ago, and Cael was still writing.

He sat in the alcove at the Ironyard's east wall, the one with the bench worn smooth by a year of practitioners doing exactly what he was doing now — sitting down right after a fight, while the details were still sharp. The alcove had no formal name. Everyone who used it regularly just called it the east bench, as you'd name a chair in your own kitchen without ever deciding to. A single lamp hung from an iron hook someone had driven into the beam above it years before Cael arrived, and the light it threw was warm and a little uneven, catching the grain of the bench where a thousand elbows had worn the wood pale.

He had twenty minutes, roughly, before the memory degraded past usefulness. He'd measured this, informally, across a year of post-bout entries — the window in which he could still recall an exchange beat by beat, before the mind began substituting the story of the fight for the fight itself. Twenty minutes when fresh. Closer to twelve when he'd taken real damage, because pain rewrote memory faster than satisfaction did. Tonight he'd taken one clean hit, second exchange, so he split the difference and wrote fast.

Beyond the alcove's low wall, the Ironyard's main floor was cycling toward its evening rhythm — the low murmur of a crowd thinning after the last scheduled bout, someone sweeping loose grit off the stone floor with the patience of a job repeated identically every night for years, Dace's voice somewhere near the entrance, negotiating tomorrow's schedule with a fighter who clearly wanted a better slot than his record currently earned him. Cael had stopped registering most of it as noise months ago. It had become, instead, something closer to weather — a condition his attention simply lived inside, the way it had once lived inside Denvash's mornings, and then Ardenmere's first strange weeks, and now this.

*Opponent: Copper-tier formal, Blade Path, six years' guild training. Watched him fight four times before agreeing to terms. Third exchange: committed strike telegraphs through a shoulder-drop his own instructors probably corrected out of him twice and never fully removed. Used it. Won in the fifth exchange.*

Beneath the summary, the working notes — the part of the entry no one else would have found legible, because it was written in the private shorthand of a method he'd built one failed assumption at a time.

*Rhythm: four breaths between committed strikes when fresh. Three when pressed. Confirmed across all four watched bouts plus tonight — five instances, no exceptions. His rhythm compresses under pressure instead of breaking. That's guild training: they drill the tempo in so deep it bends before it shatters.*

*Weight: loads the back heel a half-beat before commitment. Every time. Hands feint freely — the hands are trained liars — but the heel never once lied in five fights. Cost-to-fake rule holds: hands are cheap to fake, weight is expensive, breath under load is nearly impossible.*

*Breath: exhales on the strike, like almost everyone. Tried to disguise it in the fourth watched bout, which told me someone had warned him he was readable. The disguise was worse than the tell — he held his breath through the feint, and a held breath on a trained fighter means the strike isn't coming yet. Filed under: corrections that make the original easier to read.*

He set the pen down for a moment and let himself replay the actual fight rather than just the compressed version already living on the page — a habit he'd kept from the very beginning, checking the written account against the fuller memory while both could still be trusted.

The opponent — Ulric, a name he'd learned only because Vell's ledger required it, not because either of them had bothered with introductions — had come in confident, the settled ease of a guild-trained Blade practitioner who'd fought plenty of circuit opponents and rarely found one worth adjusting his opening approach for. Cael had let him keep that confidence through the first two exchanges, giving up ground he didn't need, letting Ulric's read of him settle into exactly the shape Ulric expected it to take. It was a technique he'd built slowly, over a year, out of watching how badly overconfidence punished itself once it finally met something it hadn't accounted for.

The shoulder-drop had shown up first in the second of the four bouts he'd watched — a small, almost invisible tell, the kind of thing that would have meant nothing without three other data points to confirm it wasn't simply an accident of that one fight. This was the watch-count rule, the oldest rule in the notebook, older than most of what surrounded it: one instance was an accident, two was a coincidence, three was structure. He never built a plan on fewer than three. He'd broken that rule exactly once, in the spring, against a Blade practitioner whose apparent weakness had turned out to be bait, and the loss it bought him still lived in the notebook, underlined, where he could find it whenever a hypothesis started feeling certain ahead of its evidence. By the fourth watched bout, the shoulder-drop had cleared the threshold with room to spare, and he'd been certain enough to build the whole plan around it: wait for the third exchange, let Ulric's guard settle into its most confident shape, and answer the telegraph a half-beat before the strike actually committed rather than after.

Then there was the part of the fight that didn't go on the tactical page at all, because it belonged to the other book.

The second exchange, first: Ulric had caught him. Cael made himself replay that one honestly, without flinching from it, because the hit was the most instructive thing Ulric had done all night. He'd been watching the hands — a lapse, a regression to the oldest and laziest layer of his own method — and the hands had lied exactly as trained hands were built to lie, a high feint that pulled his attention up while the real strike came in low and left. The impact itself he remembered less as pain than as information arriving too fast to use: a flat, jarring wrongness through his thigh, the floor briefly uncertain under him, and the small hot flush of anger at himself that he'd learned to convert, mid-fight, into attention rather than letting it curdle into recklessness. The leg had held. It was still holding now, mostly, though it had opinions about stairs.

Then the fifth exchange. The Wind-adjacent deployment.

It started, as it always started, in the hips — a drop and a loosening, as though the floor had quietly given him a half-inch it had been holding in reserve, and his weight simply stopped being where Ulric's read said it was. The breath went with it. That was the fragment's first price, paid up front, non-negotiable: it deployed on a held half-breath, and for the length of the burst he could not breathe, could not have breathed if his life had depended on the breathing rather than the moving. For that half-second he was hollow-boned, weightless in a way that had frightened him badly the first dozen times and now simply felt like a door he knew how to walk through — his body displaced a half-body-width sideways, faster than his legs had any honest right to produce, while Ulric's committed strike arrived precisely where the heel-load had promised it would and found nothing there.

Then the landing. Weight slamming back into his heels all at once, the borrowed lightness returning to ordinary mass with a jolt he'd never fully smoothed out, and one fixed half-breath of recovery during which he could not change direction — a beat of pure, locked commitment at the end of every burst, as reliable as the burst itself. He'd used that beat tonight to strike, which was the correct use of it. Someday an opponent was going to learn to wait for that beat and use it against him, and he'd been thinking about that someday with increasing seriousness for a month now.

*Wind-adjacent, one deployment,* he wrote in the margin, to be transferred to the other book later. *Cost: the half-breath, the landing beat, the usual line of strain along the left hip — crest to inner knee, deeper tonight than last time, because the burst was wider. Gain: Ulric's committed strike spent on empty air, and position on his open side while his stance was still locked. One burst bought the fight. The arithmetic continues to favor it — as long as I only need it once or twice.*

It had worked cleanly. Not dramatically — nothing about it would have looked, from the crowd's perspective, like anything more than a solid, competent circuit win. But Cael understood, in the quiet satisfaction that followed every fight built this way, that the cleanliness itself was the actual achievement. A messy win taught you less than an ugly loss. A clean win built on six hours of prior watching taught you that the method worked, again, one more time, in a fight that could have gone very differently against a less patient opponent.

Six hours was the standard now, and it had a shape. He didn't simply watch four bouts; he watched them with a division of labor he'd refined across the year, one bout per layer. First bout: rhythm only — counting the intervals between committed strikes against his own breathing, because he couldn't carry a timing glass into a crowd and his breath, seated and calm, was the most consistent clock he owned. Second bout: weight only — feet, ankles, hips, the load-path of every committed motion, ignoring the hands entirely no matter how loudly the hands demanded attention. Third bout: breath and face — the exhale patterns, the jaw, the blink he'd learned some fighters suppressed right before committing, which made the suppression itself the tell. Fourth bout: everything at once, checking whether the layers agreed with each other, promoting tells from *probable* to *confirmed* or striking them out entirely. Three columns in the notebook — confirmed, probable, discarded — and the discipline of the third column mattered as much as the first. A discarded tell, written down as discarded, couldn't sneak back into a plan; a half-remembered one could and did.

He was fifteen now. He had been in Ardenmere for just over a year, which felt like both an enormous stretch of time and no time at all, depending on which part of himself he asked. The observation notebook — the original one, the one that had started with Renn's telegraphed strikes and Dessa's transition windows — had twenty-three entries in it now, each one a little more precise than the last. The fragment log, which he'd finally separated out into its own slim volume because keeping tactical opponent data and his own private architecture in the same pages had started to feel like a filing error, had two entries. Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent.

Vell's ledger, three tables over, would already show the result: *Assessed-Copper (Hesk-ward, C.), win, atypical movement pattern in fifth exchange, opponent Copper-tier formal.* Atypical movement pattern. It was, by now, close to a permanent fixture of his entries in that ledger, the way *win by exchange-four incapacitation* had once been. Vell never asked him to explain the atypical part. He suspected she'd stopped needing an explanation months ago.

She'd caught him on the way to the alcove, actually, ledger still open, and glanced at the entry she'd just finished writing before glancing back at him.

"Ulric's going to want a rematch," she said. "He won't get one for a while. His pride needs longer to recover than his body does."

"I wasn't trying to hurt his pride."

"I know. That's usually what does it." She'd closed the ledger, no hurry in it. "You've beaten eleven Copper-tier formals this year. Every one of them thought guild training meant something a circuit fighter couldn't answer. You keep proving them wrong without ever once looking like you're trying to make a point of it. That's a specific kind of insult, whether you mean it that way or not."

"Should I apologize?"

"Absolutely not. Just don't be surprised when some of them stop being gracious about it." She'd said this without any warning in her tone, just the flat, practical observation of someone who'd watched the circuit's social currents shift for thirty years and had long since stopped being surprised by any pattern in them. "Go write your notes. I've got three more results to log before I can go home."

He'd gone. The exchange sat with him now, a small addition to the evening's accounting alongside the fight itself — one more data point in the slow, ongoing project of understanding exactly what his growing reputation was actually costing him, and what it was buying him in return.

The buying side, at least, was countable. Tonight's bout had carried a standard Assessed-tier purse: twelve silver marks posted between the two of them — eight to the winner, three to the loser, one to the ledger, which paid for Vell's ink, Vell's time, and the institutional fact of the record itself. Vell had explained the loser's share to him once, unprompted, as the single most important number in the circuit's economics: show-money kept beaten fighters coming back instead of quitting or getting desperate, and a circuit full of desperate fighters was a circuit one bad night away from a death. Dace took his venue share from the door, not the purse, which Cael had noted early as a structural choice: the man who scheduled the fights had no financial stake in who won them. Eight marks, for a night's work and six hours of watching. The boarding house cost nine marks a month for their two rooms. One clean win a month kept them housed; two kept them housed and fed and left margin for paper and ink and the occasional larger purchase. He'd run this arithmetic so many times it had stopped being arithmetic and become simply the known weight of his life, but he still ran it, most nights, because a number checked was worth two numbers assumed.

He closed the observation notebook and opened the fragment log instead, which he did rarely enough — twice a week, sometimes less — that the act itself still felt significant every time, a different kind of writing than anything else he did.

It was a slim volume, deliberately so. He'd chosen the smallest binding the stationer carried when he'd finally separated it out from the observation notebook, on the theory that a book too large would tempt him to fill it with speculation rather than confirmed fact. Two entries lived in it now, each one taking up most of a page, the surrounding white space a kind of honesty in itself — evidence of how little he actually knew, laid out plainly rather than padded with guesses dressed up as data.

He'd added a third column to each entry this month, after six months of methodically testing what the fragments could actually do rather than simply confirming, over and over, that they existed. *Functional deployment range.* It had taken him most of the winter to work out a testing method he trusted — controlled sparring sessions with Lira, logged separately from their ordinary training, each one designed to isolate a single variable rather than let the fragments blur together as they tended to under real pressure.

The method itself had gone through three failed versions before he'd settled on one that produced clean data. The first attempt — simply trying to summon a fragment deliberately, outside any fight, and timing how long it took to arrive — had told him almost nothing useful, because the fragments behaved differently under deliberate summons than they did under genuine pressure, and he'd needed weeks to understand that the difference itself was the actual finding rather than an obstacle to it. The second attempt, sparring at reduced intensity to make the fragments easier to isolate, had produced results he no longer trusted at all; a fragment tested gently didn't behave the same as a fragment tested under the kind of pressure that actually mattered in a real bout, and he'd wasted most of a month building conclusions on data that turned out to be measuring the wrong thing entirely.

The third version had worked, mostly because Lira had been the one to suggest it. Full-intensity sparring, exactly as hard as either of them could manage, with the single difference that she called a stop the instant a fragment visibly activated, giving him thirty full seconds to record what had just happened while it was still exact. It was, he'd come to understand, a method that only worked because she trusted him enough to actually fight him at full intensity in the first place, and cared enough to stop exactly when he needed her to. Neither part of that arrangement was small.

*Wind-adjacent: evasion framework, short burst. Consistent. Does not fail under pressure.* He read the line back, satisfied with it as he was satisfied with anything he'd tested thoroughly enough to state without hedging. This one he trusted completely now. He could deploy it in a fight without the small, private fear that had accompanied every early attempt — the fear that it might simply not answer this time, the way his Arbiter never had. If anything, the relationship between pressure and reliability ran backward from every other skill he owned: his footwork degraded when he was exhausted, his reads degraded when he was hurt, but the Wind-adjacent burst arrived *more* cleanly the worse the moment was, as though it had been built for exactly the half-second when everything else was failing. He'd written that observation down twice, in two different months, and both times had resisted the temptation to speculate about what it implied. The white space around the entry could hold the question a while longer.

Beneath the deployment line, the costs, itemized, because a capability recorded without its price was half a record and half a record was worse than none:

*Costs, per burst: one held half-breath (hard limit — two bursts in close succession leaves me oxygen-poor for the following exchange, and poor decisions follow poor air). Landing beat of roughly a half-breath at burst's end — locked, no direction change possible, currently exploitable by anyone who identifies it. Hip strain, leading side, cumulative — one burst is free, three in a night is a limp by morning, and the fruit vendor will notice. Ceiling: three per exchange before reliability degrades. Untested: whether the ceiling is fixed or trainable.*

The Pressure-adjacent entry sat beneath it, visibly less complete. He had a working deployment range — he knew, broadly, what it did and roughly when it tended to activate — but its upper limits under sustained combat pressure remained uncharacterized, a gap in the page that bothered him more than he usually let himself admit.

What he did know, he knew in his body more than on the page, which was itself part of what made the entry hard to finish. The Pressure-adjacent fragment gathered rather than moved — a density that arrived in the forearm and shoulder when he called for it, a sensation like his bones taking on water, weight without mass, bracing that came from inside the limb instead of from the stance beneath it. Deployed correctly, it let him take a hit that should have staggered him and stay planted — turned a strike's force into something that spread and dissipated instead of concentrating, a bad moment converted into an acceptable one. That was the gain, and it was real; he'd used it four times in bouts this year, each time to buy position at the cost of accepting contact, a trade his old self — the one who'd built an entire method around never being touched — would not have recognized as a trade at all.

The prices were steeper and slower to map. It cost more than the Wind fragment — one full deployment drained him about as much as three evasion bursts, a spreading heaviness that turned the braced arm leaden for the following two exchanges. It was slow: it needed most of a full breath to gather, which meant he had to see the hit coming well in advance, which meant it was useless against genuinely fast opponents and nearly useless as a reaction rather than a plan. And it showed. When the density gathered, his weight sank and his stance widened — a change in silhouette that a careful opponent could learn to read, an announcement that he had chosen to receive rather than evade. A fragment that telegraphed its own use. He'd underlined that line when he first wrote it, because it was the kind of price that compounded: every deployment taught the audience something.

He'd been careful about testing that boundary — the upper limit, the question of how much force the gathering could actually spread before it failed and let the full weight of a strike through. Pushing a fragment past its known limit, in the wrong setting, was the kind of experiment that could go badly enough to matter, and he hadn't yet found a controlled circumstance safe enough to justify the risk. Some questions, he'd learned, over a year of careful work, were worth leaving open a little longer than his own curiosity wanted to allow.

He was midway through annotating the Wind-adjacent entry's new column when Lira appeared in the alcove doorway, damp from her own session, and stopped.

She didn't say anything. She looked at the small, precise handwriting, the two entries, the column he was filling in — and something in her expression settled into a familiar shape: patient curiosity, held rather than spoken.

She knew the book as well as anyone but its author — the fragments had started with her, and she'd read more of its pages than anyone besides him. But he was still mid-entry, and she'd long since learned the difference between a page he offered and a page he was still writing. She waited, as she waited for most things that mattered — not passively, but with the discipline of someone who understood that some doors opened faster if you didn't lean on them.

"Good fight?" she asked instead.

"Efficient," Cael said, closing the log. "He had a tell his own instructors probably tried to train out of him. Didn't take."

"You watched him four times before you agreed to it."

"Four times is usually enough now."

Lira leaned against the doorframe, studying him with the attention she still brought to him after a year of this — not surprise anymore, just ongoing interest, the way you stay interested in a puzzle that keeps producing new pieces long after you've stopped expecting it to run out. Her hair was damp at the temples, tied back in the quick, practical knot she used for training rather than the neater one she saved for circuit days, and she still had a training weapon slung across her back that she hadn't bothered to put away before coming to find him. "You used to need six."

"I've gotten faster."

"You've gotten faster at everything." She said this without emphasis, but there was something underneath it, a genuine note of something between pride and unease that Cael had learned to recognize over the past year — the quality of someone watching a person she cared about accelerate past a pace either of them had originally planned for, and not being entirely certain, yet, whether to be glad about it or simply watchful.

She crossed the alcove and sat on the far end of the bench, close enough to see the log page without leaning over him for it, an old habit between them by now — she didn't read what he hadn't offered, and he'd learned to offer more than he used to, slowly, over the year.

"How's the leg?" she asked, nodding at the slight favoring he was doing without quite noticing he was doing it.

"Fine by tomorrow. He caught me once, clean, second exchange. My own fault — I was watching his hands instead of his weight."

"You'll fix it."

"I already wrote it down to fix it."

They'd started that morning as most mornings started now — before the market's first vendors had finished setting up, the Ironyard's side alcoves still cool and mostly empty, the two of them running through drills that had, over a year, worn as smooth and familiar as the bench Cael wrote on now.

Lira had pushed him hard, harder than usual, working a combination she'd apparently spent the previous evening designing to expose whatever gap she'd noticed in his footwork the week before. She never told him what she was testing until after — she'd decided, sometime in the last few months, that a warning changed how he moved, made him defend against the theory instead of the actual attack, and she wanted the actual attack.

"Again," she'd said, for the fourth time, after he'd failed to close the gap the same way twice.

"You could just tell me what you're looking for."

"I could. It'd be faster. It'd also teach you less." She'd reset her stance, unbothered by his mild frustration, the patience of someone who'd learned, over a year of training him, that his frustration burned itself out fast and productively if she simply let it run its course. "Again."

He'd found it on the sixth attempt — not the gap itself, which he still hadn't consciously identified, but the shape of his own hesitation right before it opened, a half-beat of over-caution that had nothing to do with his feet and everything to do with a decision he was making too slowly. He'd closed it on the seventh. She'd nodded, satisfied, and moved on to something else without further comment, the way she always moved on once a lesson had actually landed rather than merely been delivered.

That had been hours ago. His legs still remembered it, faintly, underneath the fresher ache from the evening's actual bout — the layered fatigue of a full circuit day, and he'd learned, over the year, to read its strata like anything else he studied. Morning fatigue lived in the large muscles and answered to food and water. Bout fatigue lived deeper and stranger — in the hips where the Wind-adjacent burst collected its rent, in the low buzz behind the eyes that six hours of compressed attention always left behind, a purely mental exhaustion that no one had warned him about when he'd first built a fighting method out of watching. Observation, done at the intensity he did it, was not free. After a full watched bout his eyes ached as though he'd been reading in bad light; after a fought one, the ache went all the way back into his skull. He tracked this too, in the margins. A method that ran on attention needed its fuel gauge read as honestly as any limb.

Between the two, there'd been the market, as there almost always was — the middle-hour errand that had become as fixed a fixture of the day as either training session bracketing it. He'd stopped at the dried-fruit stall first, out of a habit that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the comfort of a routine kept exactly the same for a year running. The vendor, an older woman who'd sold to him nearly every week since he'd first wandered into the district with a bag over his shoulder and no idea what any of it cost, had asked, as she always did, how the leg was healing from whatever he'd walked away favoring that week.

"You always ask that."

"You're always favoring something." She'd weighed out his usual portion without needing to be told the amount, the small economy of a transaction repeated often enough to no longer require words at all. "One day you'll surprise me and walk in here without a limp."

"I'll try."

"Don't try too hard. A boy who never limps isn't learning anything." She'd said it lightly, but there was something underneath it he recognized — an echo, faint but real, of the same principle Lira built her training around, the same one Hesk had written into a notebook years ago in a house Cael sometimes had to work to picture clearly anymore. Watch. Fail usefully. Adjust. Everyone in his life, it seemed, had independently arrived at some version of the same lesson.

He'd catalogued her stall, months ago, without meaning to — it was simply what his attention did when left unsupervised. Forty-one jars, arranged not by price but by how often the district actually bought each thing, the everyday stock at hand-height and the festival goods up where only intention could reach them. She extended credit to exactly four circuit fighters that he knew of, and he'd worked out the selection principle across a season of watching her ledger-book come out and go back under the counter: not the winners, the *consistent* — fighters whose earning she could predict, whatever their record said. It was Vell's rating system in miniature, applied to solvency instead of skill, and he suspected the two women had never once discussed it and had converged on the same architecture anyway, because it was simply what honest record-keeping looked like when it had to survive contact with a district that official systems had declined to describe.

---

Life in Ardenmere, a year on, had organized itself entirely around the circuit, in a way that felt less like a single choice made once and more like a hundred small choices compounding daily until the shape simply was what it was.

He and Lira lived in two adjoining rooms at the back of a practitioners' boarding house two streets from the Ironyard — a step up from Torvin's, paid for with circuit winnings rather than Hesk's dwindling savings pouch, which Cael had stopped touching almost entirely once his own earnings became reliable enough to make the pouch feel more like an heirloom than a necessity. His room held a bed, a desk he'd built himself out of salvaged crating with more enthusiasm than skill, and a shelf that had grown, month by month, into a small and deliberate library: the observation notebook's predecessors, retired one at a time as they filled; Hesk's leather-bound notebook, still read most weeks even though he'd long since memorized every line in it; and now the fragment log, kept apart from the others on its own end of the shelf, like something valuable set slightly out of easy reach.

Mornings were training, in the Ironyard's side alcoves before the main floor properly woke up — the hour when the light through the high windows was still thin and grey, and the only sounds were their own breathing and the occasional distant clatter of someone opening up a stall two streets over. Middle hours were the market — the fruit vendor, the baker's apprentice who'd started saving the good loaves without being asked, the quiet economy of a district that had decided, gradually and without ceremony, that the two of them belonged to it. Afternoons, more often than not, were the Ironyard itself — watching whoever was fighting on the main floor, adding entries to the observation notebook, building the kind of information advantage that had become, without him quite deciding it should, his entire method of being good at anything at all.

The Ironyard itself had become as familiar to him as Denvash's Outer District once had been, back before any of this had a name — a converted warehouse at the eastern edge of the Unranked District, three fighting spaces inside it, low ceilings and practitioner-installed lighting rigged from scavenged fixtures, and a stone floor worn smooth by years of feet doing exactly what his were doing most days now. He'd counted the lamps once, waiting out a rain delay: fourteen over the main floor, hung in a pattern that looked haphazard until you noticed it eliminated every long shadow a fighter might have hidden a tell inside, which meant someone — Dace, almost certainly — had placed them by trial across years until the floor lit evenly. The walls still carried iron rings at shoulder height from the building's wool-bale decades, and the stone underfoot had come out of a foundry's floor before it was a fighting floor, dense enough that a stamp landed dead instead of ringing. It smelled like impact dust and old iron fittings and, faintly, the mineral tang of the lamp oil Dace bought in bulk from a supplier two districts over because it burned longer than anything sold locally. Cael had stopped noticing any of it consciously months ago — except on evenings like this one, when a good fight or a strange result nudged his attention sideways long enough to catch the details fresh again, and he remembered, briefly, that this had once been a place he'd never heard of, on a night he could no longer clearly picture without effort.

They walked back to the boarding house together once the evening crowd had thinned enough to make conversation possible without shouting over it, the observation notebook and the fragment log both tucked into the same worn bag, traveling together as they had for a year even after he'd finally separated their contents onto different pages. The street outside the Ironyard was busy in its own evening way — vendors closing stalls, a pair of younger circuit hopefuls arguing good-naturedly about a bout they'd both watched from the cheap standing section, someone's dog trotting past with the unbothered confidence of an animal that had decided, long ago, this entire district belonged to it.

"You finished the deployment-range column," Lira said, not quite out of nowhere — she had a way of returning to a subject after enough silence that it felt like a fresh thought rather than a resumed one, a habit Cael had noticed months ago and never once managed to predict the timing of.

"I did."

"How long did that actually take you? Properly, I mean. Not the fight-by-fight guessing. The part where you decided you actually trusted the numbers."

"Longer than I expected. I kept wanting one more data point before I'd commit anything to the page." He said this without embarrassment — it was simply how he worked, and she'd stopped needing him to apologize for it months ago. "I trust the Wind-adjacent entry completely now. Yours, more than mine, really, given how much of the testing was just you letting me swing at you until I stopped missing."

"I didn't 'let' you do anything. You got better because I kept correcting you." She said this without heat, a familiar back-and-forth worn smooth by repetition. "The Pressure one's still not finished, though."

"No. I don't have a controlled way to push it past what a real fight already gives me, and I'm not going to manufacture a Bronze-tier opponent just to finish a column."

Lira considered this, something more serious settling under her usual directness. "Can I ask you something that isn't about the testing."

"Always."

"Does it ever bother you that I know all of it? Not just the shape — the actual fact of it. Where they come from. I was there for one of them. I've read the log more times than anyone besides you." She said it carefully, watching him for the answer rather than assuming it. "I sometimes wonder if it would be easier for you, carrying something this strange, if there were parts of it that were only yours."

Cael thought about this honestly, the way he thought about most things she asked him directly. "No," he said, finally. "It would be lonelier. I've done lonelier. I didn't like it better."

"That's a very you way of saying you're glad I know."

"I am glad you know." He said it plainly, without performance, because it was simply true and hedging it would have cost more than it earned. "I don't think I'd trust the fragments as much as I do if I'd had to build my understanding of them entirely alone. Some of what I know about them, I know because you told me what you saw from the outside, watching me use them. I don't have that view of myself. You do."

It was more than politeness. The landing beat — the locked half-breath at the end of every Wind-adjacent burst — he hadn't found that himself. Lira had, watching from three paces away in the fourth week of the winter testing, and had described it to him in the flat, useful language of a training partner rather than the careful language of a friend: *you stop being a person for a half-second at the end of it, you're just a thing that landed, and if I were fighting you for real that's when I'd hit you.* He'd written the sentence into the log nearly verbatim. Half of what the log knew about him, it knew through her eyes.

Lira studied him for a moment, something warmer than her usual directness showing through. "That's the nicest thing you've said about the notebook all year."

"It's a good notebook."

"You're supposed to say I'm the nicest thing about the notebook."

"You're not in the notebook. You're the reason half of it exists." He said this simply, and found, saying it, that it landed harder than he'd expected it to.

She bumped his shoulder with hers, easy and familiar, the gesture doing more to close whatever distance the conversation had opened than either of their sentences had managed alone. "Good answer. I'm keeping that one."

They walked the rest of the way without talking much, the comfortable quiet of two people who'd spent a year building a life together out of circuit winnings and morning drills and a shared, unspoken agreement that whatever was actually happening to Cael, they were going to face it together whenever it finally arrived. The boarding house came into view at the street's end, lamplight already lit in the front window, and Cael found himself, not for the first time, doing the small private arithmetic of how far he'd come from a fourteen-year-old standing in front of a notice board he hadn't understood yet at all.

Two entries in the fragment log. A year in Ardenmere. Eight silver marks earned tonight against nine a month in rent; a hip that would ache down its leading edge until midday tomorrow; a landing beat still unsolved; a Pressure-adjacent column still half-empty because he refused to fill it with guesses. A life that had, somewhere along the way, stopped feeling borrowed and started feeling like his own — itemized, priced, and, on balance, running at a profit.

It would have to be enough, for tonight. He suspected, without quite being able to say why, that it wasn't going to stay this simple for very much longer.

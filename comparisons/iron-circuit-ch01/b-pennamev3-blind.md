# Chapter 1 — Rated

The alcove by the east wall of the Ironyard had a bench worn smooth by forty years of fighters sitting exactly where Cael sat now, and a shelf at elbow height holding a jar of pencil stubs that nobody owned and everybody used. He had his own pencil. He always had his own pencil. But he liked the jar anyway, the way he liked most evidence that other people had sat here doing what he was doing, writing down a fight before the memory of it started lying to them.

His notebook was open to a fresh page, ruled into three columns in his own hand. Confirmed. Probable. Discarded.

Under Confirmed, first line: *Shoulder-drop before committed strike. Bouts 2, 3, 4. Live tonight, fifth exchange. Five instances.*

He sat with that line a moment longer than it needed. Five instances. His rule was three — one instance was accident, two was coincidence, three was structure — and he had built tonight's entire plan on a tell he'd confirmed at three and re-confirmed at four, and it had still cost him something to trust it when the moment came. Trusting a pattern on paper was easy. Trusting it while a Blade Path formal with six years of guild training closed the distance was a different kind of arithmetic.

The bout had been over for less than a quarter hour. Out on the main floor the second fight of the evening was already going, the crowd noise rising and falling through the low-ceilinged dark in the shape of exchanges he could read without looking: a rush, a check, a disappointed settling. The Ironyard smelled the way it always smelled at this hour, impact dust and lamp oil and the faint iron tang of the old fittings in the walls, a warehouse smell that a year of evenings had turned into something close to the smell of work itself. Three fighting spaces, stone floor polished by generations of feet, practitioner-rigged lamps that buzzed faintly when someone flared a technique too close to them. Nobody toured the Ironyard. You were either here every night or you weren't here at all.

He wrote the second line under Confirmed, and this one he wrote more slowly, because it was about himself.

*Second exchange. Watched his hands. Paid for it.*

The memory arrived the way bout memories always did when they were fresh, not as a story but as a stack of instants. Ulric's high feint, beautiful, expensive-looking, exactly the kind of motion that pulled the eye. Cael's attention going up with it — hands, always the hands, the oldest and worst of his habits — and the real strike coming in low and left while his eyes were still elsewhere. The impact had caught him on the thigh, solid enough that the leg had gone briefly stupid beneath him, and he'd spent the third exchange doing nothing but staying out of range while it decided whether it would keep holding him up.

It had held. It was holding now. It would dislike stairs for a few days, and the dried-fruit vendor in the market would clock the hitch in his stride before he reached her stall and ask after it the way she asked after everything he limped on, with the particular unhurried nosiness of a woman who had decided his upkeep was partly her business. He'd tell her a fighter caught him fair. She'd sell him the fruit anyway and overfill the bag, which was her version of medical advice.

Hands are cheap, he wrote in the margin, small, the way a man underlines something he already knows and keeps failing to know hard enough. Hands are cheap to fake. Weight is expensive. Breath under load is nearly impossible.

He flipped back six pages, because the ritual demanded it, to the entry from spring. The loss. The one time he'd broken his own counting rule, built a plan on two instances because the pattern had looked so clean, so generous, so exactly like the thing he wanted to see — and it had been bait, laid by a fighter patient enough to spend two exchanges teaching Cael a lie. He had underlined that entry the night he wrote it and the underline had gone soft and grey with re-reading. He read it again now, one full time, beginning to end. Then he turned back to tonight's page.

That was the whole discipline, really. Not the watching. Anyone could watch. The discipline was making yourself re-read your own worst night before you were allowed to enjoy your best one.

Because tonight had been good. He let himself have that, briefly, sitting on the worn bench with the crowd noise washing the dark: it had been *good*. Four of Ulric's bouts watched before Cael ever agreed to terms — six hours of standing at the rail with door-price paid and nothing to show for it that anyone else could see. Bout one for rhythm, nothing else, letting the shape of the man's timing settle into him without chasing details. Bout two for weight, which was where the shoulder-drop had first surfaced, a small forward dip a half-beat before every strike Ulric truly meant. Bout three for breath and face. Bout four for everything at once, the whole fighter assembled, the tell confirmed a third and then a fourth time until it stopped being an observation and became structure.

Six hours to learn one half-beat. It was, when he framed it that way, an absurd rate of exchange, and he knew at least a dozen fighters in this building who would laugh at it, and he had beaten most of them.

Under Probable he wrote: *Resets grip after any missed high strike. Twice tonight. Needs a third.* Under Discarded: *The foot-tap. Bout one only. Never again. Accident.* The foot-tap had looked so promising in that first watched bout that he'd almost moved it straight to Confirmed, and it had turned out to be nothing, a fighter's ankle complaining once and never again. The Discarded column existed precisely because of things like the foot-tap. It was the column that kept him honest. It was also, though he told no one this, the column he was proudest of, because any fool could collect patterns and only a careful one kept a graveyard for the patterns that died.

Then the fifth exchange, because he had put it off long enough and memory was freshest in the first hour.

He closed his eyes and let it run. Ulric pressing, confident by then, the thigh strike having told him the boy across the circle was hittable. The rhythm building toward commitment. And then the dip — the shoulder dropping that half-beat early, the tell arriving exactly on schedule like a debt being paid — and Cael's body already answering before the strike existed, the half-breath caught and held.

The Wind fragment took him sideways out of the world.

There was no other honest way to write it. Motion without the feel of moving, the burst carrying him off the line of the strike while the strike was still becoming real, Ulric's blade arm committing through the space Cael had stopped occupying. Then the landing beat: the fragment's bill, presented immediately, a half-breath in which his feet found the stone and his body locked and he could not have changed direction if the roof had come down. He'd landed on Ulric's open side. The locked half-breath had spent itself with no one there to collect on it. He'd set the edge of his hand against the seam of Ulric's ribs, politely, the way the circuit's rules preferred, and Ulric had gone still, looked down at the position, and conceded with the short exhale of a professional recognizing arithmetic.

Cael opened his eyes and wrote it plainly, and then he reached into the inner pocket of his coat and took out the second book.

The fragment log was slim, cheap-bound, three weeks separated from the observation notebook and still feeling like a new organ. Twenty-three entries in the big notebook; two in this one. It had needed to be its own volume for a simple reason he had taken almost a year to admit: the observation notebook was about other people, and its whole method assumed the subject couldn't be trusted to explain himself. The fragments were his. He was the subject. The method still applied.

Entry three, he wrote, and dated it.

*Wind. One burst, fifth exchange, deployed on the tell. Clean. Cleaner than in training. Third instance of the pattern: it arrives more cleanly the worse the moment is. Every other skill I own degrades under stress. This one sharpens. One instance was accident. Two was coincidence. Three is structure. Moving it to confirmed, and I don't like it, because I can't explain it, and a pattern I can't explain is a pattern I can't price.*

He sat looking at that line. Somewhere behind him a fighter hit the floor of the second space hard enough to draw a shout from the rail.

*Costs, tonight: half-breath held, as always. Landing beat, as always — locked, half a breath, no direction change possible. Ulric was already committed and couldn't collect. Someone who wasn't committed could. Hip: one burst, no strain worth logging. One is free. Three is a limp. Tonight I bought exactly what I could afford.*

And then, because the log's whole purpose was completeness and completeness meant writing down the choices he *didn't* make:

*Pressure: not used. Considered it in the third exchange, when the leg was deciding, and rejected it in the same breath, which is the problem — it can't be decided in a breath. It needs most of a full one to gather, so it has to be planned, never reacted. And it announces. The weight sinks, the stance widens, and any fighter worth watching reads the message: he has chosen to receive. Against a Blade Path formal who reads stances for a living, sinking into Pressure is a signed letter telling him where I'll be standing and for how long. Two entries of data. Not enough. It drains three times what Wind drains and I still can't say what it will and won't hold against. Until the data improves it stays in the column where it belongs: probable. Not confirmed. Nothing built on it.*

He closed the log. The bout was on paper now, all of it, and the strange settling that came with that arrived on schedule — the fight fully owned, fixed, no longer able to drift into a flattering story. He put both books away, flexed the disliked thigh once, and went to get paid.

---

Vell kept the ledger at a standing desk near the door, positioned so that everyone entering or leaving the Ironyard passed within arm's reach of it, which was not an accident and had never been one. She was writing when he reached her, and she finished the line she was on before acknowledging him, which was also not an accident. Vell finished things. In a district where most authority was improvised, her entire standing rested on the fact that in thirty years nobody had ever caught her ledger wrong.

"Cael." She said it the way she said all names, as a fact being checked against a record. She was past fifty, grey coming in at the temples like frost finding a seam, and she had once been Bronze-tier before a certification dispute two decades old had ended with her surrendering guild credentials rather than a point of principle. Nobody at the Ironyard knew the details. Everybody at the Ironyard knew better than to ask.

She counted out his winnings without being asked. Eight silver marks, drawn from the posted twelve — eight to the winner, three to the loser, one to the house ledger — laid on the desk in two short stacks, because Vell had opinions about the vulgarity of a single tall stack.

Eight marks. Rent for their two rooms at the boarding house ran nine a month, and he still did that arithmetic every time silver touched his palm, a habit from a year he didn't discuss: one bout, nine-tenths of a roof. He gathered the coins with the same care she'd counted them.

"Three to Ulric," Cael said. Not a question. He'd watched the loser's share paid out four times from the rail before he'd ever fought here, and he'd understood the design almost at once, and he still liked to hear it confirmed, the way some people liked to touch a solid wall.

"Three to Ulric," Vell agreed. "Beaten men who eat come back. Beaten men who don't eat get desperate, and desperate men are bad for a circuit. Dace didn't invent that rule but he's smart enough not to break it." She glanced toward the door, where the Circuit Master was doing what the Circuit Master did — counting the crowd, not the fighters. Dace took his share from the door rather than the purse, which meant he had no stake in who won anything, which meant nobody could buy an outcome through him, which was, Cael had long ago concluded, the single load-bearing honesty holding the whole building up. Dace couldn't fight and didn't pretend otherwise. He sold roofs and rules and lamplight, and business was good.

Vell dipped her pen and wrote the line. Cael had learned to read her hand upside down months ago, which he suspected she knew.

*Assessed-Copper. Win. Atypical movement pattern.*

The same third clause she'd written before. There had been a stretch, early on, when she'd asked about it — what school, what Path, what name he'd give the thing he did instead of footwork — professional questions, asked once each in her flat dry way and each time answered with something true and useless. She had stopped asking somewhere around the seventh win. Vell didn't repeat questions that didn't yield. She just wrote *atypical movement pattern* in the same steady hand, filing him under the heading of things her ledger could record without needing to understand, and he was more grateful for that shorthand than he had ever found a way to say.

"Eleven," she said, blotting the line.

"Eleven?"

"Copper-tier formals you've beaten since the year turned. Ulric makes eleven." She set the pen down and looked at him properly, and there was no heat in her face at all, which was how he knew to listen. "The first few were gracious about it. Novelty buys grace. An assessed boy with no Path and no school beats a guild man, the guild man laughs, buys a drink, tells the story on himself. Good story. But you're not a novelty anymore, you're a pattern, and guild men don't laugh at patterns. Some of them are going to stop being gracious."

"Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning nothing, exactly. That's rather the point of a warning." She turned the ledger to a fresh line, done with him in the official sense. "If I could tell you which of them and in what fashion, it would be a fact, and I'd have written it down. I deal in facts. This is the other thing. Take it anyway."

He took it. He was still arranging it into columns in his head — where did you file a warning with no instances, not accident, not coincidence, a structure someone else had seen the shape of before any of its parts arrived — when a shadow shifted at the edge of the lamplight and resolved into Ulric.

He had a cloth against the back of his neck and his kit bag on one shoulder, and he moved the way he'd moved in all four watched bouts and the fifth real one, economically, nothing spent that didn't buy something. Up close and out of the circle, he looked like what he was: a professional in his mid-twenties with six years of guild training in his posture, three silver marks in his purse, and a loss to a fifteen-year-old sitting behind his eyes in a way that had not yet decided what it wanted to become.

"A question," Ulric said. His voice was even. "The second exchange. I had you. Clean, low and left, your weight was wrong and we both knew it. Three exchanges later you were standing at my open side. I'd like to know what changed."

"I stopped watching your hands," Cael said.

It was true, and it was maybe a tenth of true, and Ulric looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a man who knew exactly what fraction he'd been served. Then he did something Cael had not put in any column, because he had never seen it from this side before.

"Four bouts," Ulric said. "Someone paid door-price at four of my bouts over two weeks and stood at the east rail and never wagered and never cheered. Dace's door-boy has a good memory for faces that don't spend. I heard about it before I ever agreed to terms with you and I thought, let the boy watch, what's watching worth." He shifted the bag on his shoulder. "Now I know what watching's worth. That was the most expensive free thing I ever gave away."

Cael said nothing. The stone under his feet felt very level and very hard.

"I'm not the aggrieved kind," Ulric said, and it had the sound of a true thing. "You beat me with preparation, which is the only way I respect losing. So take this as respect. Your next terms, whoever they're with — I'll be at the rail. I have some evenings free, and it turns out there's a boy on this circuit worth six hours of anyone's time." He nodded once, to Cael and then to Vell, unhurried, and walked out through the door's spill of lamplight into the dark, leaving behind him the precise dimensions of the thing Vell had not been able to write down.

Vell's pen scratched on, unbothered.

"That," she said, without looking up, "would be one of them."

---

Lira found him at the alcove a quarter hour later, because the alcove was where he always was after a bout and she never wasted effort searching places he wasn't. She came off the floor of the third space still breathing from her own session, knuckles taped, hair stuck to her forehead in dark commas, and she shoved his hip with hers until he surrendered enough bench for two.

"You won," she said. "Don't perform modesty, I saw the last exchange from across the floor. In on the tell?"

"In on the tell."

"Five hours of watching for one half-beat." She said it the way other people said extravagant, but she was the only person in the building who had the number nearly right, because she was the only person he'd ever shown the method whole.

"Six."

"Six hours." She leaned back against the scarred wall and closed her eyes, and for a moment they just sat in it, the crowd noise, the lamp-buzz, the smell of dust and oil, the ordinary aftermath of an ordinary working evening in the only building either of them thought of as theirs. Her session had been with the heavy post and two partners she'd talked into being unpredictable; he could read it in the specific pattern of red across her forearms. She'd tell him what she'd been drilling if he asked, or she wouldn't, depending on whether it was for her or for him.

That was the thing about Lira that had taken him longest to learn to hold correctly: half her training designs were aimed at him. She built combinations to find the gaps in him, strange asymmetric sequences with intentions she flatly refused to announce, because — as she'd told him the one time he'd pushed — a warning changes how you move, and a gap you've been warned about is a gap wearing a costume. So she tested him blind and told him afterward what she'd found, and he had hated it for about a month, and then she had found the landing beat.

He hadn't known it existed. That was the part he still turned over on nights like this one. He had used the Wind fragment dozens of times by then, logged every use, priced the held half-breath and the hip and thought his accounting complete — and Lira, watching from outside during one of her ambush combinations, had seen what no amount of interior honesty could have shown him: that at the end of every burst there was a half-breath where he landed and locked and could not answer. She'd proven it the direct way, by putting a training baton into his ribs during the lock three times in one afternoon, and then she'd stood over him while he sat in the dust and said, *you have a window painted on you, and you're the only person in the world who can't see it.*

It had gone into the fragment log that night, in his hand, from her eyes. She'd read that log more times than anyone alive but him. There was no one else who was allowed to know it existed.

"Something happened," she said now, eyes still closed. "You've got your filing face on and the bout's already filed. You file fast. So it's something after."

He told her. Vell's eleven, and the warning with no instances. Then Ulric, all of it, the door-boy's memory, the six free hours, the rail.

Lira opened her eyes.

She didn't say anything at once, and he watched her run it forward the way she ran combinations forward, seeing the sequence before it was thrown. "He watches your next terms," she said slowly. "Four bouts, if he does it the way you do it, and he's guild-trained, so assume he's at least as good an eye. What does he see?"

"Rhythm. My counting habits, maybe, if he's sharp — I look at opponents in layers and the layers probably show." He made himself say the rest, because saying it was the discipline. "And if I use Wind where he can see it more than once, he finds the landing beat. It took you three uses to be sure. Call it the same for him. It's not hidden, Lira. It's just never been watched for by anyone good."

"And it can't be trained away?"

"It's not a habit. It's the cost. The burst ends, I lock, half a breath. That's the price the fragment charges, every time, same as the held breath going in." He looked at his hands. "I can choose when to pay it. I can't choose not to."

The crowd out on the main floor groaned at something, then laughed at something else. Lira pulled the tape off her knuckles in one long thoughtful strip.

"Then we get to work on the part you can choose," she said. "When it happens. Where you land. What's standing in the window when it opens." And then, with the sideways look that always preceded the versions of her he found hardest to argue with: "Starting tomorrow. And no, I'm not going to tell you what the combinations are for."

"I know you're not."

"Because a warning changes how you move."

"I *know*," he said, and heard the year of their friendship inside the complaint, and didn't mind it being audible.

They walked home through the district's evening, the two streets between the Ironyard and the boarding house unrolling their usual inventory: supper smoke, a lamplighter cursing a stubborn wick, two children re-fighting the evening's first bout with straw and imagination, the night sounds of a place that had stopped being unfamiliar so gradually he couldn't name the month it happened. Lira spent one street on her own session — the post work, the second partner's bad habit of over-rotating, a thing she wanted him to watch for in her next bout because he'd catch it faster than she would, and he filed it, because that was the other half of the ledger between them and neither of them ever said so out loud. Their arrangement had no name and needed none. She saw him from outside. He saw her from outside. Neither of them could be trusted alone with the inside view, and both of them knew it, and that knowing was the sturdiest thing either of them owned.

The boarding house stairs took his thigh's opinion of them one step at a time, and Lira slowed to match without comment or mercy, which was her exact recipe, and he climbed through the dull protest thinking the vendor would read tomorrow's hitch from thirty feet and have the good bag filled before he reached the stall.

Their two rooms held what they held: not much, arranged carefully. Lira claimed the wash water first by ancient treaty. Cael sat at the table they'd salvaged the previous winter, lit the stub of lamp, and took out the observation notebook, meaning to close the day's accounting properly.

Twenty-three entries. Eleven Copper formals. A method that worked, and was seen to work, and had tonight been quoted back to him by a man who intended to buy it now that he knew it was for sale to anyone with door-price and patience.

He turned past the day's page to a fresh one and ruled it, slowly, into three columns. Then, at the top, in the space where an opponent's name went, he wrote his own.

He sat looking at the blank columns for a long time. Confirmed: the landing beat, courtesy of Lira, the only line he could enter, and only because someone else had seen it for him. Probable: nothing he could vouch for. The truth was he could not fill this page. He could watch anyone in Ardenmere for six hours and strip them to structure, and the one fighter on the circuit he had no way to watch was going to be studied, starting with his next terms, by a professional who now knew exactly how much watching was worth.

Water sounds from the other room, Lira humming three notes of something tuneless. He almost called through the wall to ask her — *if you were scouting me, what would you write* — and stopped with the breath already taken, because he knew her answer, and it was the correct answer, and it was already shaping tomorrow: she'd tell him nothing, and then she'd spend the next month putting batons through every window he didn't know he had painted on him, and he would find out what was on this page the way everyone else was going to find out.

The hard way. From outside.

He left the columns empty, and the name at the top, and closed the book on the page like a man setting a question where he'd trip over it in the morning.

# Brandon Sanderson — Craft Brief for Author-Harness Charter

Sanderson is the one influence in this charter who teaches his method in public,
at length, in his own words — a BYU creative-writing lecture series (English
318R), a set of essays on his own website ("Sanderson's Laws"), his official
FAQ knowledge base, and 17+ years of the Writing Excuses podcast. This brief
distills only what is publicly attested across those channels. No principle
below is invented; every one carries a source URL. Quotes are kept under 15
words and attributed.

---

## 1. Sanderson's First Law of Magic

**Statement (one sentence):** An author's ability to resolve a story's conflict
with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands the
rules of that magic.

**Operational detail — what a drafting model does differently:** Before any
scene where a character solves a problem using a special ability, the model
checks whether the ability's rules were established on the page *before* this
moment, in the same scale of detail as the solution requires. If the magic (or
tech, or skill, or resource) is being used to *create* the problem rather than
resolve it, full explanation isn't required — Sanderson explicitly frees "soft"
magic from this constraint. But if the ability is the mechanism of the
resolution, the model must be able to point to an earlier passage where the
reader could, in principle, have predicted this exact use. Tolkien's Gandalf
is cited as the soft-magic pole (rarely used to solve plot problems, rarely
explained); Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics are cited as the hard-magic pole
(fully defined, so characters can out-think a problem within them).

**Source:** [Sanderson's First Law](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law) — brandonsanderson.com

---

## 2. Sanderson's Second Law of Magic

**Statement (one sentence):** "Limitations > Powers" — what a magic system (or
any character capability) *cannot* do, and what it *costs* to use, matters
more to good fiction than what it *can* do.

**Operational detail:** The model does not stop at listing a character's
abilities; for every power granted, it must also assign at least one of three
constraint types before the ability is used in a scene: a **limitation**
(something the power flatly cannot accomplish, defining its edges — Sanderson's
example is the "Will and the Word" magic in David Eddings' Belgariad, which can
create but never destroy), a **weakness** (an external vulnerability an
antagonist can exploit — Superman and kryptonite is the reference example), or
a **cost** (a price the user pays to invoke the power — Wheel of Time
channelers going slowly insane from prolonged use is the cited example). The
craft claim is explicit: "Without limitations, there is no innovation" —
constraint is what forces a character (and a writer) toward a clever, specific
solution instead of a generic one.

**Source:** [Sanderson's Second Law](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-second-law) — brandonsanderson.com

## 3. Sanderson's Third Law of Magic

**Statement (one sentence):** Expand what you already have before you add
something new — depth beats breadth in worldbuilding and magic-system design.

**Operational detail:** Before the model introduces a new named ability,
faction, technology, or magic subsystem in a chapter, it must first check
whether an *existing* established element could be extended, recombined, or
given a new consequence to serve the same narrative need. The essay's guidance
is to "extrapolate" (ask what a society would actually do with an established
power — e.g., a power to change skin color at will reshaping religion and
social class), "interconnect" (tie existing powers/cultures/themes together
rather than let them sit in isolation), and "streamline" (combine near-
duplicate elements into one stronger one). The negative example cited is
*Spider-Man 3*, criticized for piling on new villains instead of deepening the
one it already had.

**Source:** [Sanderson's Third Law of Magic](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-third-law-of-magic) — brandonsanderson.com

## 4. Sanderson's Zeroth Law

**Statement (one sentence):** Rules exist to serve wonder, not the reverse —
"always err on the side of what's awesome."

**Operational detail:** This is the override clause on Laws 1–3: if rigorous
adherence to established rules would produce a flat, mechanical, or
joyless scene, the model is permitted to bend toward the more striking image
or moment, provided it doesn't break reader trust built by the hard-magic
contract (Law 1) in a scene the reader is relying on that contract for. In
practice this means the Zeroth Law governs tone and spectacle decisions
(is this magical moment awe-inspiring on the page), while Laws 1–3 govern
structural fairness (can the reader follow and eventually predict the system).
Sanderson explicitly modeled the "Zeroth Law" framing on Asimov's own Zeroth
Law of Robotics, which likewise sits above and can override the numbered laws
beneath it.

**Source:** Arcanum (Sanderson quote archive), Shadows of Self San Diego
signing, Oct. 8, 2015 — [wob.coppermind.net](https://wob.coppermind.net/adv_search/?query=Zeroth); corroborated by [Sanderson's Laws of Magic overview](https://coppermind.net/wiki/Sanderson%27s_Laws_of_Magic) — coppermind.net

*(Note: this law does not appear in Sanderson's own numbered blog-essay
series on brandonsanderson.com — it originates from signing-event and Q&A
remarks archived by the fan-run but Sanderson-endorsed Arcanum quote database.
Flagged here as secondary-sourced, unlike Laws 1–3 which are primary essays.)*

## 5. Promise, Progress, Payoff — as a chapter-level discipline

**Statement (one sentence):** Every unit of story — book, act, chapter, scene
— should open with an implicit promise to the reader about what kind of
satisfaction is coming, spend its middle showing measurable progress toward
that promise, and close by either paying it off or visibly advancing it.

**Operational detail:** A chapter's opening paragraph functions as what
Sanderson's 2025 lecture calls a "stealth thesis paragraph" — it signals, without
announcing outright, what tone and type of conflict this chapter is about to
deliver. A drafting/editing model should be able to name, for any chapter's
first page, the specific promise being made (a mystery to chase, a
relationship to test, a fight to survive). At the chapter's last page, the
model checks whether that promise was paid off outright, or whether the
chapter instead delivered visible progress toward it — Sanderson's own
diagnosis of reader drop-off names missing "signposts of progress" as the
cause. A chapter that ends having done neither — no payoff, no legible
progress — is a structural defect under this model, independent of prose
quality.

**Source:** [Brandon Sanderson's 2025 Guide to Plot (Lecture #2)](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2) — brandonsanderson.com

## 6. Foreshadowing mechanics

**Statement (one sentence):** Payoffs that matter should be seeded well before
they land — Sanderson foreshadows major reveals across books, not just
scenes, and treats under-foreshadowing as a fixable craft failure he has
personally made.

**Operational detail:** On timing, Sanderson describes building payoffs a
reader can retroactively trace back across volumes — wondered about in a
later book, then visible as planted "hints" on rereading the first. He
names his own Mistborn-trilogy shortfall as the cautionary case — plot
elements at the end of book one "should have been better foreshadowed" and
didn't get what they deserved — and states that with the Stormlight Archive he
became "much more careful with foreshadowing" as a direct correction. For
antagonist-created problems specifically, he names the failure mode "Deus Ex
Wrench" (coined with colleague Bryce Moore): a sudden magical complication
sprung on the reader with no groundwork feels exactly as cheap as a sudden
magical *solution* would. The fix is the same in both directions — the threat
must have been walked past earlier, or something parallel to it must have
already happened on the page. A drafting model applying this should track, per
major late-book reveal, at least one earlier passage that plants it, and treat
a reveal with zero earlier touches as a red flag rather than a "surprise."

**Source:** [How Do You Use Foreshadowing?](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/how-do-you-use-foreshadowing/) and [How Do You Apply Your Three Laws Of Magic To Villains?](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/how-do-you-apply-your-three-laws-of-magic-to-villains/) — faq.brandonsanderson.com

## 7. The hard-magic reader contract

**Statement (one sentence):** In a hard-magic (or any strongly rule-governed
capability) system, the reader must be able to see a solution coming from
rules already on the page — a solution invented from a rule introduced only
at the moment of use is a cheat, not a twist.

**Operational detail:** This is Law 1 restated as a *contract*, and it is the
single most citable, checkable Sanderson principle. When a character resolves
a problem using an established-rules ability, the correct reader reaction
Sanderson is aiming for is "the solution was available all along — they just
didn't see the application," not "where did that power come from." His
diagnostic question for a drafting pass is explicit: for any hard-magic
problem, ask "how could the characters use what they already have and know to
solve this conflict?" — and then make them do that, rather than granting a
new ability in the moment. A model enforcing this contract should be able to
flag, for any climactic use of an established-rules capability, whether an
earlier passage taught the reader the specific mechanism now being exploited;
if the answer is no, the solution is either miscategorized (it should have
been written as soft magic, exempting it from this contract) or it is a
contract violation that needs a revision pass.

**Source:** [Sanderson's First Law](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law) — brandonsanderson.com; corroborated by [How Do You Apply Your Three Laws Of Magic To Villains?](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/how-do-you-apply-your-three-laws-of-magic-to-villains/) — faq.brandonsanderson.com

## 8. The MICE Quotient, as Sanderson teaches it

**Statement (one sentence):** Every story is a mix of four possible promise
types — Milieu, Inquiry, Character, Event — and the type that *opens* a given
narrative thread is the type whose resolution is owed to the reader before
that thread can close.

**Attribution note:** The MICE Quotient originates with Orson Scott Card, not
Sanderson; Sanderson teaches it as inherited craft apparatus in his BYU
lectures and it is taught at length on Writing Excuses (primarily by Mary
Robinette Kowal, Sanderson's longtime co-host). It is included here because
the task specifies "as he teaches it," and Sanderson does actively use and
assign the framework in his own course.

**Operational detail:** The four types, as commonly taught in this lineage: a
**Milieu** thread opens when a character enters a new space and closes when
they leave it (the promise is "how do we get out/through here"); an
**Inquiry** thread opens with a question or mystery and closes when it's
answered; a **Character** thread opens with a character's internal
dissatisfaction and closes with either a resolution or an accepted new status
quo; an **Event** thread opens when the status quo is disrupted and closes
when a new status quo is established (Sanderson's own Mistborn is cited as a
canonical Event-type epic-fantasy structure). A model using MICE as a
structural check should identify, for any subplot, which letter it opened as,
and confirm the thread is closed with the matching payoff type — an Inquiry
thread that "resolves" via a character's internal acceptance rather than an
answer is a MICE mismatch, not a valid ending.

**Source:** [Writing Excuses 6.10: Scott Card's M.I.C.E. Quotient](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-6-10-scott-cards-m-i-c-e-quotient/) and [16.35: What is the M.I.C.E. Quotient?](https://writingexcuses.com/16-35-what-is-the-m-i-c-e-quotient/) — writingexcuses.com; referenced in Sanderson's own [2025 Lecture #1](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-writing-class-2025-week-1) — brandonsanderson.com

## 9. Hard vs. soft magic as a spectrum, not a binary

**Statement (one sentence):** Magic systems (and by extension any
extraordinary character capability) sit on a continuum between fully
rule-defined ("hard") and deliberately mysterious ("soft"), and the position
on that continuum — not the presence of magic itself — determines whether it
may be used to *solve* problems on the page.

**Operational detail:** Sanderson's own guidance for soft-magic writers is
blunt: "rarely use their magic to solve problems." A model should classify
each supernatural or extraordinary element in a manuscript on this spectrum
before drafting scenes that use it, because the classification changes which
rule (Law 1's contract, or the looser soft-magic exemption) applies to it.
Sanderson cites Harry Potter as a series that manages a blended position: each
individual book is "generally cohesive" (harder, more locally rule-bound)
while the overarching series magic remains mysterious and expanding across
seven books (softer at the macro scale). This means the hard/soft
classification is not fixed per-franchise — it can and should be assessed at
the scale (single scene / single book / whole series) actually in play.

**Source:** [Sanderson's First Law](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law) — brandonsanderson.com

## 10. Worldbuilding — the "hollow iceberg"

**Statement (one sentence):** Build the sense of a vast world by developing
key, story-relevant elements deeply and letting everything else stay
implied — not by exhaustively detailing everything that could theoretically
exist.

**Operational detail:** Sanderson's own framing corrects the popular
"iceberg theory" — writers are not actually building a solid ten-times-larger
mass beneath the visible tenth; they're building a **hollow** iceberg, doing
just enough work in the unseen portion that a reader senses depth without the
writer having fully engineered it. The craft instruction that follows: ground
the reader in sensory, concrete detail ("describe how it feels") rather than
in abstract exposition, because concrete cues let a reader infer deeper rules
"organically" instead of being told them. He explicitly reframes the goal as
relational rather than volumetric: "It's not about density of ideas. It's
about how the ideas influence each other." A model applying this should
prefer letting worldbuilding surface through a character's sensory experience
of a scene over standalone expository passages, and should not treat an
un-elaborated background detail as a defect — the gap is the intended effect.

**Source:** [Worldbuilding Tools from Brandon Sanderson | Lecture Notes 2025](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025) — brandonsanderson.com

## 11. Revision philosophy

**Statement (one sentence):** Sanderson treats drafting and revision as
distinct, sequential jobs — the first draft exists to get raw material into
place, and every subsequent pass has a distinct, narrowing purpose, across a
process he describes running 8–12 passes deep.

**Operational detail:** By his own account the passes specialize: the first
draft is "generally focused on getting things in place" without concern for
polish, including letting character voice drift and only fixing it in a later
pass rather than mid-draft; the second draft is where he "settle[s] on how I
want things to really look, feel, and work" — i.e., structural and
characterization decisions lock here, not earlier; a subsequent pass targets
prose compression specifically, with a stated goal of cutting the manuscript
by roughly 10% for pacing and vividness; later passes are editorial and
alternate between structural and line-level work. He also keeps revision notes
as a separate, organized artifact (sorted "by character and by importance")
rather than folding them into the draft in progress. The operational takeaway
for a drafting/editing pipeline: don't ask a single pass to fix voice,
structure, and prose density simultaneously — those are Sanderson's own
distinct passes, and collapsing them is a process error, not efficiency.

**Source:** [What Is Your Process For Drafting A Book?](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/what-is-your-process-for-drafting-a-book/) — faq.brandonsanderson.com

## 12. Architect vs. gardener (outlining posture)

**Statement (one sentence):** Sanderson's own method is a hybrid he calls
"points on the map" — a loose, revisable outline of major waypoints, with
discovery-writing filling the connective material between them.

**Operational detail:** He directly credits *more* outline structure with
his *improved* Stormlight-era foreshadowing versus early Mistborn (§6) — so a
model choosing "outline first" vs. "draft first" per project should treat
outline density as a dial connected to foreshadowing risk, not a style
preference.

**Source:** [Can You Go Into Depth About Outlining?](https://faq.brandonsanderson.com/knowledge-base/can-you-go-into-depth-about-outlining/) — faq.brandonsanderson.com

## AS ENFORCEABLE GATES

Converting the strongest principles above into checks an editor (human or
automated) could actually run against a chapter or manuscript — grep-able,
countable, or citable. No vague virtues; each gate names a pass/fail
condition and where to look.

**Gate 1 — Hard-magic solution traceability (Law 1 / §7).**
For every scene where a character resolves the chapter's central conflict
using an established ability, rule, or resource: locate the earliest prior
passage that states the specific mechanism now being used. FAIL if no such
passage exists AND the ability is not explicitly classified as soft magic
(§9) for this story. Check: search backward from the resolution scene for the
capability's name/mechanism; record chapter/page of first mention; compare to
resolution chapter.

**Gate 2 — Limitation/weakness/cost tagging (Law 2 / §2).**
For every named special ability used on-page more than once: confirm at
least one of {limitation, weakness, cost} has been stated in prose before or
at its second use. FAIL if an ability has ≥2 uses with zero stated
constraint anywhere in the manuscript. Check: build a table of ability name →
first constraint mention chapter; flag blanks.

**Gate 3 — New-element vs. existing-element ratio (Law 3).**
Count newly introduced named magic systems / factions / unique-tech elements
per act against the count of prior-established elements given new
consequences or connections in that same act. FAIL if new-element
introductions exceed existing-element extensions by more than 2:1 within a
single act. Check: tag every named "new capability/faction" mention by
first-appearance chapter; bucket by act; compute ratio.

**Gate 4 — Chapter promise/payoff closure (§5).**
For every chapter: state the promise implied by its opening page in one
sentence; state whether the closing page delivers a payoff, a progress beat,
or neither. FAIL any chapter scored "neither." Check: two-column log,
opening-page promise vs. closing-page delivery type, filled for 100% of
chapters — zero blank rows permitted.

**Gate 5 — Foreshadow-before-reveal (§6).**
For every reveal flagged as "major" (changes the reader's understanding of
plot, antagonist capability, or world rule): locate at least one earlier
passage that plants it, per the "Deus Ex Wrench" standard — either the
character walked past it, or a parallel event already happened on-page. FAIL
if a major reveal has zero prior plants. Check: reveal list cross-referenced
against a plant list; every reveal ID must have ≥1 plant ID with an earlier
chapter number.

**Gate 6 — MICE thread-type match (§8).**
For every subplot/thread: tag its opening type (M/I/C/E) and its closing
type. FAIL if the closing type doesn't match the discipline the opening type
requires (Milieu → exit-the-space; Inquiry → question-answered;
Character → dissatisfaction-resolved-or-accepted; Event → new status quo
established). Check: thread ledger with open-type, close-type, match/mismatch
column.

**Gate 7 — Iceberg ratio / exposition-vs-sensory audit (§10).**
For every worldbuilding passage exceeding 150 words: classify each sentence
as sensory/concrete (grounded in a character's perception) or abstract/
expository (told directly to the reader). FAIL any passage where expository
sentences exceed sensory sentences 2:1 or greater. Check: sentence-level
tagging per long worldbuilding block; compute ratio; flag violators.

**Gate 8 — Revision-pass separation (§11).**
For any manuscript claiming "final" or "polished" status: confirm the
project's revision log shows at least three distinct passes with distinct
stated purposes (e.g., structure/voice lock, prose-density cut, line edit) —
not one undifferentiated "revised" pass. FAIL if the log shows a single
pass covering structure, voice, and prose density simultaneously with no
separation. Check: revision log entries must each declare one primary target
(structure | voice | density | line-edit); a pass declaring more than two
simultaneous targets fails this gate.

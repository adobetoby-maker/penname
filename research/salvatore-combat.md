# Salvatore Combat Craft Brief

Research scope: publicly available interviews, his own craft blog post, and secondary
craft-analysis pieces about R.A. Salvatore's fight-scene technique. No fiction text was
ingested or quoted. All direct quotations below are under 15 words and are marked with
quotation marks; everything else is paraphrase/summary of sourced claims. Principles
that are this researcher's inference from secondary analysis (not Salvatore's own
stated words) are explicitly labeled INFERRED.

Compiled: 2026-08-30. Purpose: input to an author-harness charter — a set of
operational, testable rules a drafting model and an editor-model can both apply to
combat prose.

---

## 1. Physical literacy is the foundation, not the flourish

**Statement:** Combat choreography reads as authentic only when it is grounded in how
bodies actually move, not in a mental image borrowed from other fantasy prose.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Before generating any fight, the model
should establish (in its own working notes, not necessarily on the page) each
combatant's stance, footwork, and center of balance, and should treat "balance" as the
primary constraint on every action a fighter takes — a fighter who is unbalanced
cannot simultaneously attack, and prose should reflect that trade-off beat by beat.
Salvatore himself credits hockey, boxing-club training, work as a bouncer, and close
observation of tai chi, yoga, and ice dancing for this instinct — the throughline
across all of it, in his account, is that balance is the most important part of any
fight, more about footwork than hand-work.

**Source:** https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/314-r-a-salvatore-on-combat-and-dungeons-dragons

## 2. Sentence length is a tempo dial, and short is his stated default for speed

**Statement:** Salvatore's own explicit craft advice is that sentence and paragraph
length should compress as a fight accelerates — shorter units read faster and raise
pulse.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** During a rising-action combat beat, the
model should progressively shorten sentence length and paragraph length as the
exchange intensifies, down to single-sentence paragraphs or fragments at the peak of a
clash. This is not a stylistic garnish — Salvatore describes it as his direct
mechanism for pace: "my sentences get shorter, paragraphs become single sentences."
A drafting model should treat mean sentence length in a combat passage as a
controllable, checkable variable that tracks intensity, not a fixed prose register.

**Source:** https://www.tumblr.com/blogsguide/8944705696/ra-salvatore-on-how-to-write-a-damn-good-fight
(mirror of Salvatore's Goodreads blog post "How to Write a Damn Good Fight Scene")

---

## 3. The verb "to be" is treated as the first casualty of a fight scene

**Statement:** Salvatore names passive/stative "to be" constructions (is, was, were,
had been) as the single most damaging drag on combat momentum, and states he
deliberately purges them from action beats.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** In any passage tagged as active combat,
the model should default to active, tensed action verbs and should treat a rising
density of "was/were/is/being" as a signal to rewrite the sentence around a concrete
verb of motion or impact instead. This is checkable mechanically (a simple word-count
audit of "to be" forms per 100 words of combat prose, expected to trend toward zero at
the peak of an exchange) and should be built into the harness as a lint-style check,
not just a style suggestion.

**Source:** https://www.tumblr.com/blogsguide/8944705696/ra-salvatore-on-how-to-write-a-damn-good-fight ;
corroborated independently at https://www.apexbookcompany.com/a/blog/apex-magazine/post/interview-with-r-a-salvatore

---

## 4. Rhythm variation, not uniform shortness, is what a close read of his prose shows

**Statement:** A secondary craft-analysis piece that studied Salvatore's actual combat
passages (as opposed to his own stated advice) argues his practice is more nuanced
than "always go short" — he sometimes runs longer, breathless sentences during a
sustained clash specifically to deny the reader a place to pause, then snaps to one or
two single-sentence paragraphs for a shock beat.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** INFERRED (secondary analysis, not
Salvatore's own statement) — the harness should not treat "shorten every sentence"
as a universal rule but should model two distinct tempo registers: (a) a sustained,
comma-linked, breath-denying long sentence for disorientation/immersion during a
prolonged exchange, and (b) an abrupt one-line paragraph immediately after it for a
turn, a landed blow, or a reversal. The contrast between the two registers, not either
one alone, is what produces the felt acceleration. An editor-model should flag combat
passages that are uniformly short throughout as likely under-varied.

**Source:** https://fantasyhandbook.wordpress.com/2014/12/02/pacing-an-action-scene/
(third-party craft analysis naming Salvatore as the studied example)

## 5. The battlefield itself is treated as a choreography generator, not a backdrop

**Statement:** Salvatore states that after decades of writing duels, his own
preparation shifted from planning move sequences to planning terrain — because a
fresh environment forces fresh, non-repeating choreography.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Before generating a fight, the harness
should require a terrain/geography spec — not flat, open ground by default, but a
specific constraint set (a narrow cave mouth, a rocky slope, an opponent with a
non-humanoid body plan) — and should require that at least two distinct combat beats
in the scene be caused by that terrain (a fighter using a ledge for leverage, a cramped
space forcing a weapon switch, uneven footing costing someone their balance).
Environment-as-backdrop with no mechanical effect on the fight should fail review.

**Source:** https://www.tumblr.com/blogsguide/8944705696/ra-salvatore-on-how-to-write-a-damn-good-fight ;
corroborated at https://www.apexbookcompany.com/a/blog/apex-magazine/post/interview-with-r-a-salvatore

## 6. Per-fighter style is built to characterize, not just to differ mechanically

**Statement:** Salvatore designs each major fighter's combat style as an expression of
personality and worldview — Drizzt's acrobatic, dance-like dual-blade technique;
Dahlia's segmented, transforming weapon enabling rapid style-switching (explicitly
credited to the armory duel in *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon*); Pwent's pure
offense-and-rage close-quarters brawling.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Every named combatant in the story bible
should carry a combat-style profile (footwork pattern, preferred range, risk tolerance,
signature tactic) that is derived from that character's established personality traits,
not assigned independently of them. The drafting model should be required to write
that profile before the first fight scene involving the character and to check new
combat beats against it for consistency, the same way it would check dialogue voice.
A cautious, calculating character should not suddenly fight with reckless abandon
without in-scene justification, and vice versa.

**Source:** https://www.apexbookcompany.com/a/blog/apex-magazine/post/interview-with-r-a-salvatore ;
corroborated at https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/314-r-a-salvatore-on-combat-and-dungeons-dragons

## 7. Technical precision serves momentum; it does not replace it

**Statement:** Salvatore aims for combat mechanics that hold up to a reader who knows
fighting, but his stated priority in the moment of drafting is emotional immediacy —
he describes mentally "watching" the fight as he writes it rather than working from an
exhaustive technical script, and keeps action moving rather than pausing to explain it.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Technical fight terminology (named
stances, formal maneuver names, weapon-specific jargon) should be rationed — used only
where it does double duty as both accurate and evocative — and should never be allowed
to stall the sentence-level pace established by rule 2. A secondary craft source
studying fight-scene writing broadly (not Salvatore specifically, but citing his work
as the standard to emulate) makes the same case explicitly: minimize jargon, because
technical accuracy that requires the reader to stop and decode a term has failed the
scene even if it is correct.

**Source:** https://www.apexbookcompany.com/a/blog/apex-magazine/post/interview-with-r-a-salvatore ;
supporting craft argument at https://www.swantower.com/2010/11/16/writing-fight-scenes-introduction/
(names Salvatore's Drizzt/Entreri duels as a formative influence, while separately
arguing for restrained jargon — this pairing is the researcher's synthesis, marked
INFERRED where it extends beyond what either source states alone)

## 8. Stakes and reader investment must exist before the first blow lands

**Statement:** Salvatore has stated that a fight only works if the reader already cares
about the combatants — the fight is not the source of investment, it is the
expression of investment that must already be present.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** The harness should block a drafting model
from opening a combat scene "cold" — i.e., between characters or in a stakes context
the reader has not yet been given reason to care about. A pre-combat checklist item:
name at least one concrete thing (relationship, goal, unresolved grievance, or
survival need) established on the page before this fight that the outcome of this
specific fight will affect. If that checklist item cannot be filled in, the scene is
not ready to be drafted as combat yet — it needs setup first.

**Source:** https://www.apexbookcompany.com/a/blog/apex-magazine/post/interview-with-r-a-salvatore

---

## 9. Injury and consequence must persist — combat outcomes are not reset between scenes

**Statement:** Cited craft analysis of Salvatore's approach (and his own framing of the
difference between physical and magical combat) treats lasting physical consequence as
what keeps stakes real — a fireball that instantly ends a fight removes the dramatic
tension that graduated, persistent injury provides in blade-to-blade combat.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Any injury sustained in a combat scene
should be logged as continuity state and referenced at least once in subsequent scenes
(altered mobility, visible wound, fatigue, a favored side now guarded) until it is
explicitly resolved (healed, bound, or narratively closed). An editor-model check:
search forward from any "wounded" beat for the next appearance of that character and
confirm the injury either persists or its resolution is shown on the page — injuries
that silently vanish between scenes should fail review.

**Source:** https://fantasy-faction.com/2015/writing-fight-scenes (craft analysis citing
Salvatore as exemplary, discussing magic-vs-physical combat consequence) ;
corroborated at https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/314-r-a-salvatore-on-combat-and-dungeons-dragons
(Salvatore on avoiding "bigger monster next book" escalation by keeping his
protagonist "running as much as fighting" rather than simply scaling threat upward)

## 10. Not every fight gets full treatment — scene weight should vary

**Statement:** Salvatore does not choreograph every clash at the same resolution;
significant duels receive full stance-by-stance, tactic-by-tactic treatment, while
minor or narratively unimportant skirmishes are compressed to a sentence or a
paragraph.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** Before drafting any fight, the harness
should require a one-word weight classification (major / minor) tied to plot
significance and character stakes (rule 8), and should scale word count and mechanical
detail accordingly — a minor skirmish rendered at major-duel length is a pacing defect,
not a bonus. This prevents the common failure mode of a drafting model treating combat
description as inherently valuable regardless of story weight.

**Source:** https://www.tumblr.com/blogsguide/8944705696/ra-salvatore-on-how-to-write-a-damn-good-fight

## 11. Escalation discipline: harder fights should be tactically different, not just numerically bigger

**Statement:** Salvatore states he actively resists the standard fantasy-series
escalation trap (bigger monster, higher numbers, next book) and instead raises
difficulty by changing the tactical problem — for example, having his protagonist
evade and outmaneuver during an orc-war arc rather than simply facing stat-bigger
enemies head-on.

**Operational detail for a drafting model:** When a story calls for a "harder" fight
than a previous one, the harness should require the difficulty increase to come from a
changed tactical variable (numbers requiring retreat-and-pick-off tactics, terrain
denying a favored technique, an opponent who counters the protagonist's signature
move) rather than from raising an abstract power number. A reviewer check: can the new
fight's core tactical problem be stated in one sentence that is different from the
previous fight's tactical problem? If not, the escalation is numeric only and should
be reworked.

**Source:** https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/314-r-a-salvatore-on-combat-and-dungeons-dragons

## Note on "fights as conversations/arguments" framing — NOT SOURCED

This framing was searched for directly (multiple query variants targeting Salvatore
specifically using "conversation" or "argument" language about combat) and was not
found attributed to Salvatore in any available public material. The closest adjacent,
independently sourced idea is that his recurring antagonist pairing (Drizzt and
Artemis Entreri) is built on an explicit ideological/personal clash that plays out
through repeated, evenly matched duels across multiple books — but no source frames
this as Salvatore himself describing fights as "conversations" or "arguments" between
characters. Per instructions, this principle is omitted from the numbered list above
rather than asserted on a guess. If the charter wants this framing, it should be
sourced to a different craft tradition (it appears more commonly in screenwriting/stage
combat pedagogy) rather than attributed to Salvatore.

## AS ENFORCEABLE GATES

Concrete, mechanically checkable gates an editor (human or model) can run against a
drafted combat scene. Each ties back to a numbered principle above.

1. **Drawable geography gate (→ principle 5).** An editor should be able to sketch a
   rough top-down diagram of combatant positions and terrain features at any paused
   point in the scene using only the prose. If positions cannot be reconstructed
   without guessing, the scene fails this gate.

2. **Tempo-rhythm correspondence gate (→ principles 2, 4).** Chart mean sentence
   length across the scene in sequence. It should trend downward into the climax of
   an exchange (principle 2) and should contain at least one deliberate long-vs-short
   contrast pair around a turning point (principle 4). A flat, uniform sentence-length
   profile across the whole scene fails this gate.

3. **"To be" density gate (→ principle 3).** Count stative "to be" forms per 100 words
   in the combat passage. Density should be measurably lower than the surrounding
   non-combat prose and should trend toward zero at the peak beat. High density during
   the height of an exchange fails this gate.

4. **Per-fighter style distinctness gate, masked-name test (→ principle 6).** Strip
   character names and pronouns from the action beats (replace with "Fighter A" /
   "Fighter B") and have a reader (or a second model) attempt to reassign each beat to
   the correct combatant using only tactics, footwork, and word choice. Below a set
   accuracy threshold (e.g., unable to correctly reassign the majority of beats), the
   scene fails — it means the fighters are mechanically interchangeable.

5. **Stakes-before-blow gate (→ principle 8).** Confirm, in the text preceding the
   first exchange of blows, at least one concrete, page-established reason a reader
   should care about the outcome for at least one combatant. Absence fails the gate
   and the scene should not proceed to drafting as combat until setup is added.

6. **Injury persistence gate (→ principle 9).** For every wound/injury/exhaustion
   state introduced in the scene, confirm it recurs or is explicitly resolved in the
   character's next appearance. Injuries that are never referenced again fail this
   gate.

7. **Scene-weight calibration gate (→ principle 10).** Confirm the classification
   (major/minor) assigned before drafting matches the actual word count and
   mechanical density delivered. A minor skirmish drafted at major-duel resolution (or
   the reverse — a major duel compressed to a sentence) fails this gate.

8. **Environmental utilization gate (→ principle 5).** Confirm at least two distinct
   combat beats in the scene are causally dependent on a specific terrain or
   environmental feature (not merely mentioned as set dressing). A scene where the
   environment could be deleted with zero effect on how the fight unfolds fails this
   gate.

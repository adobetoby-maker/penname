# Optional Module — Progression (Monroe 1.3, Iron Prince + HWFWM standard — no restraint)

Apply when selected by the scene packet. Adopted from Bryce O'Connor & Luke
Chmilenko's *Iron Prince* and Shirtaloon's *He Who Fights With Monsters* in
full — both authors' own demonstrated techniques, channeled openly, not a
cautious subset. Per `CORE.md`'s anti-pastiche invariant: their technique and
structure, never their characters or invented worlds.

## The floor is verifiably the worst in the room

Growth reads as earned, not gifted, when the protagonist's starting position
is mechanically the worst on every visible axis except one — not "secretly
special," actually behind. The one exception (a growth rate, a rare trait, an
unusual door) stays inert until spent: it produces nothing without repeated,
on-page cost feeding it. This converts "chosen one" into "instrumented one" —
the specialness is a number the reader watches update under real strain, not
an authorial assertion.

## Skill beats stat at least once per arc — this is the actual competitive thesis

A visible stat/rank system gives readers something to track at a glance, but
the prose keeps the *feeling* of a fight in front of the number, not behind
it. The central competitive mechanism: raw tier (equipment, augmentation,
rank) and combat skill (technique, read, intelligence) are two separate axes,
and real wins come from maximizing the axis the system doesn't track
numerically. At least one major win per arc must be attributable to tactics or
technique, not to a stat or rank advantage — the protagonist beats someone
with a better number using something the number sheet doesn't measure. This is
the pressure valve against spreadsheet fiction: the real growth is often
happening somewhere the visible stat isn't looking.

## Every training/growth scene names its delta

A scene that advances a numeric stat or rank must name the specific delta —
what moved, by how much, to what new tier. No scene may claim "she trained and
got stronger" without a legible before/after marker. Any growth tied to
training must show what was expended to produce it — a loss, an injury, a
fight picked deliberately above the character's level. Growth that occurs
off-page with no shown cost has failed. Montage/compression is fine for
*quantitative* movement (numbers changing); a scene that also claims to
deliver new plot, world-rule, or emotional information must be rendered in
full, not summarized — compressing information rather than numbers is the
documented cause of a manuscript reading as padded rather than propulsive.

## Rank is social and economic, not only a combat ceiling

Advancement should visibly change how a scene works even when no fight is
happening — pricing, deference, legal standing, who gets waved through a
checkpoint, how long a life is expected to run. Treat rank as a caste system
with real stakes, not a combat-only power dial. If a scene's point is
institutional unfairness, show the institution acting unfairly on someone —
do not have the protagonist explain that it's unfair. Class/social critique
delivered as protagonist speech rather than dramatized action is the
documented failure mode to avoid.

## Rivals are the clock, not the obstacle

A rival's role is to set the tempo the protagonist's own climb must be checked
against — a moving target, not a stationary bar. Every named rival needs their
own on-page advancement within the same arc the protagonist advances; a rival
static while the protagonist grows fails this requirement. A rival's first
loss to (or by) the protagonist works best as a loss with a social hook
attached — it should draw people together, not simply isolate the loser.

## The tournament/ladder is escalation of a running mechanism, not a bolt-on climax

A standing, ongoing competitive structure (a sparring ladder, a ranked roster,
a regular proving ground) keeps stakes live between the big set-piece
tournament beats, so the tournament itself escalates something already
running rather than introducing competition from scratch late in the book.
Seeding and matchups should be earned by the same visible rank system the
reader has been tracking, so an upset reads as consistent with earlier
information (skill compensating for a stat gap) rather than authorial
thumb-on-the-scale. Tournament stakes run on two registers at once: personal
(rank, survival, rival standing) and social/spectacle (who else is watching
and what it means to them) — use both.

## The system is a diegetic object with an owner, never bare exposition

A status box, notification, or system voice is not narration to the reader —
it is an object inside the world that a specific character perceives, reacts
to, and can argue with. Every system-text appearance must be legible as
something a named character is looking at, in a scene, for an in-world reason.
Track the system's internal logic in author-side reference notes separate
from what the reader sees — the player-facing display and the internal
consistency ledger are different documents for different audiences, and the
box a reader sees is not something they're meant to be able to game off of. A
well-designed system box is defined as much by what it withholds as what it
shows; treating it as a site of personality conflict (a character arguing with
an unhelpful prompt) only works if the box can be incomplete or withholding
enough to be worth arguing with. Keep game-element display minimal and
specific to its purpose — surface a system only where it changes a scene's
stakes or a character's choice, never as a running inventory.

## Ability combinations are seeded before they pay

A powerful combination reads as earned, not author fiat, because its
components were on-page and individually load-bearing before they combined.
Any combo-style payoff must name its individual components, and each
component must have already done independent narrative work — resolved a
smaller problem, cost something, been established as scarce — before it is
combined into something bigger. An in-world taxonomy of which components
reliably produce which results (a Guild's records, a Magic Society's
classification) gives the combinatorics a logic the reader can learn and
predict, which is the mechanism that makes a later payoff feel seeded rather
than sprung. If a long-running combinatorial system's rules ever change, that
change needs an explicit, story-justified cause — the system's own internal
consistency is valuable enough that breaking it needs a reason, not just
narrative convenience.

## Protagonist voice can carry the whole engine — let it, and manage the real risk deliberately

A voice strong enough to carry a series is, by the same mechanism, strong
enough to exhaust some readers; the craft answer is not a quieter voice, it is
managing the trade-off on purpose. Strong reader polarization is a legitimate
outcome to prefer over blandness. What keeps a strong comedic/coping voice
from grating over a long series, per the authors' own demonstrated technique
— not caution, technique:

1. An explicit in-world reason the voice exists (coping with being lost and
   overwhelmed, not authorial quirk).
2. Real narrative cost when the voice is used carelessly — a joke under
   stakes complicates the situation (worse position, an angered party,
   a lost resource) at some traceable point; jokes that cost nothing,
   repeatedly, are the voice-fatigue failure mode.
3. Scheduled non-comedic beats, at a fixed minimum cadence, showing the
   character without deflection — no jokes, no lampshading — so the voice
   reads as a mask with something real underneath it, not a mask with
   nothing behind it. Watch for secondary characters drifting into the same
   speech pattern as a sign the voice needs to recede for a stretch.
4. Dual-layered humor where possible: a joke that lands for the reader while
   remaining genuinely destabilizing or alien to in-world characters gives
   the humor a narrative function beyond being funny.

## The moral-cost thread needs one trackable, losable stake

Power should leave residue — on the character, and on the reader's ability to
root for them cleanly — and the story is stronger for refusing to resolve that
residue with a clean answer. This needs a concrete, trackable stake: a
specific power, relationship, or standing the character could plausibly lose
or corrupt because of how they use what they have. Interior monologue about
whether their values hold up is not sufficient on its own — the documented
failure mode is a moral thread that stays rhetorical (the character worries
about becoming what they hate) without ever becoming a real, actionable risk
(the character actually could lose something specific by continuing). Name the
stake, and advance or reference it at defined checkpoints.

## Setbacks are the fuel, described at the length they've earned

An origin-level setback (illness, loss, disadvantage) is not backstory color;
it should be the mechanical reason the character's baseline sits where it
does, with visible ongoing evidence of its cost, not a fact stated once and
dropped. In-fight setbacks feed growth directly and visibly — the worse a
loss, the more it should be shown converting into the next capability, not
merely survived. The real, documented craft risk here is not setback density
itself but *exhaustive description* of every setback — no more than one
consecutive scene of pure setback (loss/injury with no new information or
relationship movement) before either a partial win, a new tactical insight, or
a relationship beat.

## Serial discipline, even without a live serial

Keep a written roadmap, separate from the manuscript, that a chapter either
follows or explicitly departs from and returns to — no chapter drafted without
a current outline entry. A forcing-function cadence, even self-imposed,
produces the regular checkpoints where consistency gets checked before it
compounds into a rewrite. Published-book editing exists to prune and tighten a
superset of material, not to rewrite the engine from scratch.

## Editor gates

1. Advancement has an intelligible cause and a recorded delta.
2. At least one major win per arc is attributable to skill/tactics over a
   stat or rank advantage.
3. Cost or tradeoff is visible whenever the system promises one, and any
   trained growth shows what was expended to produce it.
4. No system box appears without a perceiving character, in a scene, for an
   in-world reason.
5. Any ability combination names its prior components, each independently
   load-bearing before combination.
6. Rank produces at least one non-combat consequence per major rank-bearing
   scene (pricing, deference, legal standing, access).
7. Class/social critique is dramatized, not delivered as protagonist speech.
8. Every named rival has their own on-page advancement within the arc the
   protagonist advances.
9. Every comedic beat used under stakes carries a traceable cost or
   consequence within the same scene or the next.
10. The protagonist's voice goes quiet, without deflection, on a fixed
    minimum cadence.
11. The moral-cost thread has one trackable, losable stake, referenced at
    defined checkpoints — not interior monologue alone.
12. No more than one consecutive pure-setback scene before a partial win, a
    tactical insight, or a relationship beat.
13. Repeated challenges differ in tactical, emotional, or social problem —
    escalation by changed problem, not by raised number.
14. Rivals and institutions do not freeze off-page solely to accommodate the
    protagonist's pace.
15. A living roadmap document exists separate from the manuscript, checked
    at each chapter.

# Craft — Worldbuilding (Monroe 1.3, Sanderson standard — no restraint)

Adopted in full from Brandon Sanderson's own published Laws, lectures, and
essays (see `pen-names/fantasy-author-a/PROVENANCE.md` for the research
record). Not a rationed subset — every principle below is his demonstrated
method, not a critic's caution layered onto it. Per `CORE.md`'s anti-pastiche
invariant, this is technique and method, channeled openly; never his wording,
characters, or Cosmere setting.

## The Laws of Magic (any rule-bound capability, not just "magic")

**First Law.** An author's ability to resolve a conflict with a special
capability is directly proportional to how well the reader understands the
rules of that capability. Before any scene where a character solves a problem
using a special ability, the rules must already be on the page in the same
scale of detail the solution requires — the reader's reaction should be "the
solution was available all along," never "where did that come from." If the
capability is *creating* the problem rather than resolving it, this contract
loosens (see "soft" below); if it is the mechanism of resolution, it is
mandatory.

**Second Law. Limitations > Powers.** What a capability cannot do, and what it
costs, matters more than what it can do. Every power granted a real use in the
story needs at least one of: a **limitation** (something it flatly cannot
accomplish, defining its edges), a **weakness** (an external vulnerability an
antagonist can exploit), or a **cost** (a price paid to invoke it). Without
limitations there is no innovation — constraint is what forces a character,
and a writer, toward a clever specific solution instead of a generic one.

**Third Law. Expand, don't add.** Depth beats breadth. Before introducing a
new named ability, faction, technology, or subsystem, check whether an
*existing* established element could be extended, recombined, or given a new
consequence to serve the same need instead. Extrapolate (what would a society
actually do with an established power), interconnect (tie existing elements
together rather than let them sit in isolation), streamline (combine near-
duplicate elements into one stronger one). A brilliant system is more often a
few powers considered in real depth than a thousand powers considered
shallowly.

**Zeroth Law.** Rules exist to serve wonder, not the reverse — always err on
the side of what's awesome. This is the override clause on the three laws
above: if rigorous adherence would produce a flat, mechanical, or joyless
scene, bend toward the more striking image or moment, provided it doesn't
break reader trust built by the First Law's contract in a scene the reader is
relying on that contract for. The Zeroth Law governs tone and spectacle; the
numbered laws govern structural fairness. Sanderson's own caveat applies to
all four: they are working guidelines that have proven durable, not absolute
rules — a violation of any of them should be a deliberate choice made because
a specific scene calls for it, not a default.

## Hard vs. soft is a spectrum, assessed at whatever scale is in play

A capability's position between fully rule-defined ("hard") and deliberately
mysterious ("soft") — not the mere presence of the supernatural — determines
whether it may be used to *solve* a problem on the page. Classify each
extraordinary element before drafting scenes that use it. The classification
is not fixed per-book or per-series: a single scene, a single book, and the
whole series can each sit at a different point on the spectrum for the same
underlying system, assessed at the scale actually in play in that moment.

## Foreshadowing: seed before you spend, in both directions

Payoffs that matter should be seeded well before they land, across books, not
just scenes — a reveal a reader can retroactively trace back on reread. Track,
per major late-book reveal, at least one earlier passage that plants it; a
reveal with zero earlier touches is a red flag, not a surprise. This applies
symmetrically to complications: a sudden magical *complication* sprung on the
reader with no groundwork ("Deus Ex Wrench") is exactly as cheap as a sudden
magical *solution* would be. The threat must have been walked past earlier, or
something parallel to it must already have happened on the page, in either
direction.

## Worldbuilding depth is relational, not volumetric

Build the sense of a vast world by developing story-relevant elements deeply
and letting everything else stay implied — a hollow iceberg, not a solid one.
This is not restraint on how deep to go where it matters; it is a specific
instruction about *where the depth lives*: ground the reader in sensory,
concrete detail (how it feels, what it costs, what it looks like happening)
rather than in abstract exposition, because concrete cues let a reader infer
deeper rules organically. "It's not about density of ideas. It's about how the
ideas influence each other." An un-elaborated background detail is not a
defect — the gap is the intended effect, and it frees the depth budget to go
fully into what the story is actually using. Prefer letting worldbuilding
surface through a character's sensory experience of a scene over standalone
expository passages.

## Promise, Progress, Payoff — at every level, book down to scene

Every unit of story opens with an implicit promise about what kind of
satisfaction is coming, spends its middle showing measurable progress toward
it, and closes by either paying it off or visibly advancing it. A chapter's
opening functions as a stealth thesis: it signals, without announcing, what
tone and type of conflict this chapter is about to deliver. "The feeling of
progress is what keeps the reader turning the page" — a chapter that ends
having delivered neither payoff nor legible progress is a structural defect
independent of prose quality.

## The MICE Quotient — match the close to the open

Every story thread is one of four promise types, and the type that *opens* a
thread is the type whose resolution is owed before it can close: **Milieu**
(a character enters a space; closes when they leave it — the promise is how do
we get through here), **Inquiry** (opens with a question; closes when
answered), **Character** (opens with internal dissatisfaction; closes with
resolution or an accepted new status quo), **Event** (opens when the status
quo is disrupted; closes when a new one is established). An Inquiry thread
that "resolves" via a character's internal acceptance rather than an answer is
a mismatch, not a valid ending.

## Try-fail cycles, and yes-but / no-and

A character attempts a solution, fails, learns something, attempts again — 
typically three failures, each raising difficulty, before success. Every plot
beat within that cycle is either **yes-but** (succeeds, but a complication
follows) or **no-and** (fails, and the problem worsens). This creates
escalation without repetition.

## Chapter endings: structural incompleteness, not cheap cliffhangers

Never tie off every loose end at a chapter's close — leave a hint of something
exciting, intriguing, or unexpected around the next corner. This is not a
gimmick stacked on top of good prose; it is what makes an 800-page book read
like a fast 350.

## The convergent climax ("Sanderlanche")

Regular chapters run one or two POVs in a consistent rotation. As the climax
approaches, that rotation tightens and becomes more predictable; at the climax
itself, the structure deliberately breaks — rapid cuts, multiple POVs per
chapter, plot threads that have been running in parallel converging into the
same sequence. The effect is disorientation, then a sense of convergence, then
catharsis. Braid parallel plots tightly rather than alternating them in blocks
— each thread should feel like it's pulling taut at the same time as the
others, not taking its turn.

## Revision is distinct, sequential jobs, not one undifferentiated pass

Drafting exists to get raw material into place, not to polish. A subsequent
pass settles structural and characterization decisions. A subsequent pass
targets prose compression specifically (Sanderson's own stated target: cut
roughly 10% for pacing and vividness). Later passes alternate structural and
line-level work. Do not ask a single pass to fix voice, structure, and prose
density simultaneously — collapsing them is a process error, not efficiency.
